\focusbanner{TODAY'S PROBLEM}

Suppose 50 student volunteers test a planning prompt for a logic task. They are randomly assigned to two groups of 25. Mean times are 14.2 minutes with the prompt and 17.8 with standard directions: prompt minus standard is $-3.6$. Under no effect, a simulation shuffles the same times into new groups. Only 18 of 2{,}000 shuffled differences are $-3.6$ or lower.\par\smallskip \textbf{Nia:} ``This is evidence that the prompt lowered mean time for these volunteers, but we could still be wrong.''\par \textbf{Owen:} ``The $0.009$ is the probability of no effect, so there is a $99.1\%$ chance the prompt works for every district student.''\par

\end{center}

\begin{callout}{\IconBook\ ASK WHAT CHANCE WOULD DO UNDER NO EFFECT (keep on the page)}{calloutgreen}
Under no treatment effect, the expected difference in means is 0. A rerandomization simulation
keeps the observed responses and group sizes, randomly reassigns the responses, and repeats.
The fraction at least as extreme in the claim's direction estimates how often chance alone
would give such a result. It does not give the probability that no effect is true.
A small fraction is evidence against chance alone, but errors remain possible.
Random assignment supports causation for the study subjects; random sampling supports population claims.
\end{callout}
