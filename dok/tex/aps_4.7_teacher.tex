\textbf{Expected elements}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{reconciles-mean-and-mode} (required) --- Explains how a 1.50-dollar mean gross payout can coexist with an 87.5 percent chance of no prize
  \item \textbf{uses-net-expectation} (required) --- Subtracts the ticket cost and uses the negative 0.50-dollar expected net to assess the money-making claim
  \item \textbf{bounds-one-buyer-meaning} (required) --- Interprets expected value as a long-run average and states that it does not predict an individual ticket's outcome
  \item \textbf{defends-buying-decision} (required) --- Makes a decision tied to an explicit financial or nonfinancial criterion rather than treating expected value as a command
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Common mistakes}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item Forgetting to subtract the \$2 ticket cost from each prize
  \item Averaging the four possible dollar values without probability weights
  \item Treating \$1.50 as the most common payout
  \item Interpreting an expected loss of \$0.50 as a guaranteed outcome for every buyer
\end{itemize}

\focusbanner{TODAY'S PROBLEM --- annotated}

Suppose a club sells 200 raffle tickets for \$2 each. One ticket wins \$100, four tickets win \$25 each, 20 tickets win \$5 each, and the other tickets win nothing. Let $X$ be the buyer's net profit from one randomly chosen ticket: prize minus ticket cost.\par\smallskip \textbf{Priya:} \emph{The average gross payout is \$1.50 per ticket, so buying one is a good deal.}\par \textbf{Mateo:} \emph{Most tickets win nothing.}\par
\begin{center}
\textbf{Ticket net-profit distribution}\par\smallskip
\begin{tabular}{|l|c|c|}
\hline
\textbf{Prize} & \textbf{Net $x$} & \textbf{$P(X=x)$} \\ \hline
\textbf{Top prize} & $98$ & $1/200$ \\ \hline
\textbf{Second prize} & $23$ & $4/200$ \\ \hline
\textbf{Small prize} & $3$ & $20/200$ \\ \hline
\textbf{No prize} & $-2$ & $175/200$ \\ \hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\begin{firsttakebox}{FIRST TAKE (5 min)}
Would you buy one ticket if your goal were to make money? Use one number or outcome from the problem.\par
\answer{Not graded. Students may focus on the top prize or on the 175 losing tickets; the distribution should connect those views.}
\end{firsttakebox}

\textit{Rules callout ``DISTRIBUTION, EXPECTATION, AND ONE OUTCOME (keep on the page)'' is printed on the student sheet.}\par\medskip

\dokbadge{1}~\textbf{(a)} State the four possible values of $X$ and identify the most likely value.\par
\answer{The possible net profits are $98$, $23$, $3$, and $-2$ dollars. The most likely value is $X=-2$, because 175 of the 200 tickets pay no prize.}

\dokbadge{2}~\textbf{(b)} Verify that the probabilities form a distribution. Calculate the expected gross payout and the expected net profit $\mu_X$.\par
\answer{The probabilities sum to $1/200+4/200+20/200+175/200=200/200=1$. Expected gross payout is $100(1/200)+25(4/200)+5(20/200)+0(175/200)=1.50$ dollars. Expected net profit is $\mu_X=98(1/200)+23(4/200)+3(20/200)-2(175/200)=-0.50$ dollar per ticket. Equivalently, $1.50-2.00=-0.50$.}

\dokbadge{3}~\textbf{(c)} Decide whether Priya's numerical statement and Mateo's statement can both be true, and explain why there is no contradiction. Assess Priya's good-deal conclusion using the ticket cost and $\mu_X$. Explain what expected value does and does not predict for one buyer, then decide whether you would buy and defend the criterion behind your choice.\par
\answer{Both numerical statements are true. The mean gross payout is $1.50$ dollars even though $175/200=87.5\%$ of tickets pay no prize, because the few large prizes pull the mean above the typical payout of zero. Priya's good-deal conclusion is not supported as a money-making claim: after the \$2 cost, the long-run average net is a loss of $0.50$ dollar per ticket. Expected value predicts the average net over many repetitions under this distribution; it does not say one buyer will lose exactly $0.50$ dollar. One ticket yields only $98$, $23$, $3$, or $-2$ dollars. A student may reasonably decline on financial grounds, or buy if supporting the club and entertainment are worth more than the expected loss, provided that nonfinancial criterion is explicit.}
