Suppose 120 students answer two yes-or-no questions: ``Do you like pizza?'' and ``Do you like tacos?'' The table summarizes their answers. Let $P$ be the event that a randomly selected student likes pizza and $T$ the event that the student likes tacos.\par\smallskip \textbf{Jamie:} ``$P(P\text{ or }T)=80/120+60/120=140/120$. Because a probability cannot exceed 1, this must mean everyone likes at least one; I would report the answer as 1.''\par \textbf{Noor:} ``Some students may have been counted in both totals, so we need the intersection before deciding.''\par

\textit{Rules callout ``INTERSECTIONS AND MUTUALLY EXCLUSIVE EVENTS (keep on the page)'' is printed on the student sheet.}\par\medskip

\dokbadge{1}~\textbf{(a)} State the number in $P\cap T$ and write its probability as a fraction.\par
\answer{The intersection contains 35 students, so $P(P\cap T)=35/120=7/24\approx0.292$.}

\dokbadge{2}~\textbf{(b)} Find the probability that a selected student likes at least one food, counting each student once. Also find the probability of liking neither.\par
\answer{There are $35+25+45=105$ students who like at least one food, so the probability is $105/120=7/8=0.875$. There are 15 who like neither, with probability $15/120=1/8=0.125$.}

\dokbadge{3}~\textbf{(c)} Are the two events mutually exclusive, and whose reasoning is supported? Use the overlap and your probability to explain Jamie's error. Explain what reporting everyone would hide. Write 3--4 sentences.\par
\answer{The events are not mutually exclusive because 35 students like both foods, so Noor's reasoning is supported. Jamie counted those 35 twice: $80+60-35=105$ students like at least one, giving $105/120=0.875$. Reporting everyone would hide the 15 students who like neither; a probability above 1 signals a counting error.}
