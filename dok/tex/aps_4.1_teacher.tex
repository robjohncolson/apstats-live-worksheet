\end{itemize}

\textbf{E} & Chooses Claim B with the 7-of-100 evidence, gives a complete matched simulation with many trials, and identifies a very small stable proportion as evidence against fairness without claiming the current result proves fairness. \\ \hline
\textbf{P} & Makes the supported claim choice and gives most of the simulation, but omits the at-least-6 event, a large repetition count, or a clear result that would challenge fairness. \\ \hline

\textbf{Common mistakes}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item Treating a run of six as impossible because each individual sequence is unlikely
  \item Using six flips rather than reproducing all 10 flips in one trial
  \item Counting only runs of exactly 6 and discarding longer runs
  \item Claiming that 7 percent proves the coin is fair
\end{itemize}

\textit{Rules callout ``MODEL THE PATTERN, NOT THE STORY (keep on the page)'' is printed on the student sheet.}\par\medskip

\dokbadge{1}~\textbf{(a)} State the longest run in the observed sequence and the most common longest-run value among the simulated sets.\par
\answer{The observed sequence has a longest run of 6, the six consecutive heads. In the simulated sets, 3 is the most common longest-run value, appearing 32 times.}

\dokbadge{2}~\textbf{(b)} Use the dotplot to estimate the probability that 10 fair-coin flips have a longest run at least as long as the observed run. Interpret the estimate in context.\par
\answer{Seven of the 100 simulated sets have a longest run of at least 6, so the estimate is $7/100=0.07$. In context, about 7 percent of sets of 10 independent flips from a fair coin produced a run at least as long as the observed run in this simulation.}

\dokbadge{3}~\textbf{(c)} Which claim is better supported by this evidence? Cite the simulation result that settles your choice. Then describe a larger simulation that would more precisely settle the disagreement: state what one trial is, what to record and count, how many trials to use, and what result would make you doubt that the coin is fair.\par
\answer{Claim B is better supported. The fair-coin simulation produced a run of 6 in 7 of 100 trials, so a streak this long is possible by chance; 100 trials give only a rough estimate and do not prove the coin fair. For a stronger simulation, use independent equally likely H/T outcomes. One trial is 10 flips; record the longest run of either outcome and mark the trial a success when that run is at least 6. Repeat at least 10,000 trials and compute the successful-trial proportion. A stable proportion extremely close to zero---for example, below 1 percent---would make the observed run very unusual under the fair-coin model and provide reason to doubt fairness. A proportion near the displayed 7 percent would not.}
