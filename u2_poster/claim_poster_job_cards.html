Content objective

I can judge a claim about chance: find the number it really depends on, prove my verdict with it, and repair the claim so it is true.

Poster 1 — Who wants to be rich?

The claim you are judging

The student newsletter wrote: "Freeze Time people are the ones who want to be rich. 26 of them chose Rich, more than any other superpower."

Solve together first. Scratch paper and whiteboards, no poster yet.

  1. For each superpower, what percent of its fans chose Rich? Write a fraction and a percent. Which total is the denominator, and why?
    Answer. Fly 22/89 = 24.7% · Freeze Time 26/101 = 25.7% · Invisibility 22/98 = 22.4% · Super Strength 13/77 = 16.9% · Telepathy 19/68 = 27.9%. The denominator is the row total: "given Fly" means only the 89 Fly students count. Overall, P(Rich) = 102/433 = 23.6%.
  2. Draw a segmented bar chart: one 100% bar per superpower, split into Famous / Happy / Healthy / Rich. Whole percents are fine. Add a key and a percent axis.
    Answer (percents within each superpower: Famous / Happy / Healthy / Rich). Fly 5.6 / 53.9 / 15.7 / 24.7 · Freeze Time 3.0 / 62.4 / 8.9 / 25.7 · Invisibility 3.1 / 63.3 / 11.2 / 22.4 · Super Strength 5.2 / 55.8 / 22.1 / 16.9 · Telepathy 0.0 / 72.1 / 0.0 / 27.9. Overall 3.5 / 61.2 / 11.8 / 23.6.
  3. Verdict. Do Freeze Time fans stand out for wanting to be rich? Compare their percent with the other four groups and with all 433 students.
    Answer. No. The count is right (26 is the largest Rich count), but Freeze Time is also the largest group (101 fans), so a large count is expected. By percent Freeze Time is 25.7%, close to the 23.6% of all students. Telepathy is highest at 27.9% (Freeze Time is second, 2.2 points behind), and every group sits between 17% and 28%. No group stands out: not supported. Also accept the true reading of the count: of the 102 students who want Rich, 26 (25.5%) chose Freeze Time, more than any other power. That is P(Freeze Time | Rich), not the claim's P(Rich | Freeze Time).
  4. Repair. Rewrite the claim so it is true. Use a percent, not a count.
    Answer (any true version). "About 1 in 4 students in every superpower group wants to be rich (17% to 28%); Freeze Time fans, at 25.7%, are close to the 23.6% of all students." Also true: "Among students who chose Telepathy, 27.9% want to be rich, the highest share; Freeze Time is second at 25.7%."
  5. Is there an association between superpower and status? Compare two bars that look different (try Telepathy and Fly) and explain in one sentence.
    Answer. Yes: the conditional distributions differ. Telepathy fans: 0% Famous, 0% Healthy, 72% Happy. Fly fans: 5.6% Famous, 15.7% Healthy, 54% Happy. With no association every bar would be split the same way.

The claim you are judging

A headline in the school paper: "Most students who want to be Famous chose Fly. So most students who chose Fly want to be Famous."

Solve together first. Scratch paper and whiteboards, no poster yet.

  1. Find P(Fly | Famous) and P(Famous | Fly) as fractions and percents. Which total is the denominator each time, and why?
    Answer. P(Fly | Famous) = 5/15 = 33.3%: the 15 students who want to be Famous (a column total). P(Famous | Fly) = 5/89 = 5.6%: the 89 students who chose Fly (a row total). Same numerator, different "given" group.
  2. Shade the "given" group on two copies of the table, one for each probability. Circle the cell the two share.
    Answer. Copy 1: the Famous column shaded (total 15) for P(Fly | Famous). Copy 2: the Fly row shaded (total 89) for P(Famous | Fly). The shared cell is 5: same numerator, different given group.
  3. Verdict on each half. Is "most" right in the first sentence? Does the second sentence follow from the first?
    Answer. First half: Fly is the top pick among the 15 Famous students (5; next is Super Strength with 4), but 33% is not "most": a plurality, not a majority. Second half: it does not follow; the condition is flipped. Only 5.6% of Fly-choosers want to be Famous.
  4. Repair. Rewrite the headline as two true sentences, each with its probability.
    Answer. "Among the 15 students who want to be Famous, Fly was the most popular power (5, or 33%). Among the 89 who chose Fly, only 5.6% want to be Famous."
  5. Build. Find another pair in the table where flipping the "given" changes the answer a lot. Write both probabilities and explain in one sentence why they differ.
    Answer (any correct pair). P(Happy | Super Strength) = 43/77 = 55.8% vs P(Super Strength | Happy) = 43/265 = 16.2%. P(Happy | Freeze Time) = 63/101 = 62.4% vs P(Freeze Time | Happy) = 63/265 = 23.8%. P(Rich | Telepathy) = 19/68 = 27.9% vs P(Telepathy | Rich) = 19/102 = 18.6%. Why: the numerator is the same cell; the denominator is the size of the given group.

Poster 3 — Can't happen together, so unrelated?

DOK 3

Data

Super Status! 433 high school students answered: "What superpower would you choose?" and "What status would you prefer?" One of these 433 students is picked at random.
SuperpowerFamousHappyHealthyRichTotal
Fly548142289
Freeze Time363926101
Invisibility362112298
Super Strength443171377
Telepathy04901968
Total1526551102433

The claim you are judging

"Nobody who chose Telepathy wants to be Famous: 0 of 68. Since the two never happen together, Telepathy and Famous are independent. One has nothing to do with the other."

Solve together first. Scratch paper and whiteboards, no poster yet.

  1. Are "chose Telepathy" and "wants to be Famous" mutually exclusive? Show the joint probability P(Telepathy and Famous).
    Answer. P(Telepathy and Famous) = 0/433 = 0. No student in this table did both, so for a student picked from this table the two events are mutually exclusive. (The 0 shows it did not happen among these 433, not that it never could.)
  2. Are they independent? Compare P(Famous) with P(Famous | Telepathy).
    Answer. P(Famous) = 15/433 = 3.5% but P(Famous | Telepathy) = 0/68 = 0%: not equal, so not independent. (The product check agrees: P(Famous) × P(Telepathy) = (15/433)(68/433) = 0.0054, but P(Famous and Telepathy) = 0.)
  3. Verdict, in one sentence that uses both terms correctly. What does knowing "Telepathy" do to the chance of "Famous"?
    Answer. "Telepathy and Famous are mutually exclusive but not independent: knowing a student chose Telepathy drops the chance they want to be Famous from 3.5% to 0." Seeing one rules the other out, which changes its probability: that is dependence, not independence.
  4. Prove it in general. Two events can each happen, and they are mutually exclusive. Explain why they can never be independent. (What does independence say P(A and B) must equal?)
    Answer. Independent means P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B). If both events are possible, that product is above 0. Mutually exclusive means P(A and B) = 0. Both cannot be true at once, so mutually exclusive events with positive probabilities are never independent.
  5. Build. Make a 2x2 table of 100 students for "plays a sport" and "plays an instrument" where the two events are independent but NOT mutually exclusive. Write the check under it.
    Answer (one of many). Independent: sport 60, instrument 50, both 30 → sport only 30, instrument only 20, neither 20. Check: P(instrument | sport) = 30/60 = 50% = P(instrument) = 50/100. Any table with both = P(S) × P(I) × 100 works. Extension for a fast group: a second table where the events ARE mutually exclusive (sport 50, instrument 50, both 0, neither 0); the same check fails, 0/50 = 0% against P(instrument) = 50%.

Poster 4 — Double counted

DOK 3

Data

A survey of 2,500 adults asked about education level and where they get their news. One of these 2,500 adults is picked at random.
GroupCount
College graduates693
Get news mainly from the internet687
College graduates who get news from the internet245
All adults surveyed2,500

The claim you are judging

A blog wrote: "P(college grad or internet news) = 693/2500 + 687/2500 = 55.2%. And college grads are no more likely than anyone else to get their news online: only 245/2500 = 9.8% of adults are grads who read news online."

Two mistakes are hiding in this claim. Find both.

Solve together first. Scratch paper and whiteboards, no poster yet.

  1. Draw a Venn diagram (or a two-way table) and fill in all four regions: both, grad only, internet only, neither. Check that they add to 2,500.
    Answer. Both 245 · grad only 693 − 245 = 448 · internet only 687 − 245 = 442 · neither 2,500 − 1,135 = 1,365. Sum 2,500.
  2. Find the mistake in the 55.2%. Compute the correct P(grad or internet). Which people were counted twice?
    Answer. The 245 adults who are both were counted in 693 and again in 687. P(grad or internet) = (693 + 687 − 245)/2500 = 1135/2500 = 45.4%. (The blog's 1380/2500 = 55.2% is a union with double counting.)
  3. The blog backs its second sentence with 9.8%. Is 9.8% a joint or a conditional probability? Which probability does "no more likely than anyone else" really depend on?
    Answer. 9.8% = 245/2500 = P(grad and internet), a joint probability out of all 2,500 adults. "No more likely than anyone else" compares grads with everyone, so it depends on the conditional P(internet | grad), set beside P(internet).
  4. Test the second sentence with the right probability. Are the two events independent? Who is more likely to read news online?
    Answer. P(internet | grad) = 245/693 = 35.4% vs P(internet) = 687/2500 = 27.5%: not equal, not independent. Grads are MORE likely to read news online (35.4% vs 27.5% overall, and vs 442/1807 = 24.5% of non-grads). The blog's second sentence is false. (The product check agrees: P(grad) × P(internet) = 0.2772 × 0.2748 = 0.0762 vs P(both) = 245/2500 = 0.098.)
  5. Build. Keep 693 college grads and 687 internet readers. How many people in "both" would independence predict? Compare that with 245. What does the gap tell you?
    Answer. both = 693 × 687 / 2500 = 190.44 people, about 190. The survey found 245, about 55 more than independence predicts, so the dependence is large, not rounding. (190.44 is not a whole number, so tables of these sizes can never be exactly independent.)

Poster 5 — Is the game fair?

DOK 3

Data

A carnival game costs $2 to play. You roll a fair six-sided die. Roll a 6: you win $10. Roll a 1: you win $3. Otherwise: you win nothing.

The claim you are judging

The sign on the booth: "A FAIR GAME! You win on 2 of every 6 rolls."

Solve together first. Scratch paper and whiteboards, no poster yet.

  1. Let X = the player's net profit for one game (winnings minus the $2). List every value of X with its probability in a table. Check that the probabilities add to 1.
    Answer. X = +8 (roll 6) with 1/6 · +1 (roll 1) with 1/6 · −2 (roll 2, 3, 4, 5) with 4/6. Sum 6/6 = 1. Using $10, $3, $0 instead of net is the first trap.
  2. Compute the mean μX and the standard deviation σX. Show the formula and the substitution; a calculator may do the arithmetic.
    Answer. μX = 8(1/6) + 1(1/6) + (−2)(4/6) = (8 + 1 − 8)/6 = 1/6 ≈ $0.17. σX² = (8 − 1/6)²(1/6) + (1 − 1/6)²(1/6) + (−2 − 1/6)²(4/6) = 485/36 ≈ 13.47, so σX ≈ $3.67. Shortcut: E(X²) = (64 + 1 + 16)/6 = 13.5, minus μ² = 1/36.
  3. Interpret μX and σX in context. Use "in the long run" and say "per game".
    Answer. In the long run, over many games, a player gains about 17 cents per game on average. A single game's net typically differs from that average by about $3.67.
  4. Verdict. Is the game fair? Who does it favor? Why is "win on 2 of every 6 rolls" the wrong number for judging fairness?
    Answer. A fair game has μX = 0, for both sides. Here μX = +$0.17, so the game favors the PLAYER: the booth loses about 17 cents per game in the long run. "2 of 6" is the chance of winning something on one roll (not a promise of two wins in six). It counts how OFTEN you win, not how MUCH: the $10 prize outweighs the four $2 losses.
  5. Repair. Change ONE payout so that μX = 0 exactly. Show the new table and the new mean.
    Answer (either). The 6 pays $9 (net +7): μ = (7 + 1 − 8)/6 = 0. Or the 1 pays $2 (net 0): μ = (8 + 0 − 8)/6 = 0. Raising the price to $13/6 ≈ $2.17 also balances it (exactly only at $13/6) but is not a payout change.
  6. Simulate 20 games. On the TI-84: randInt(1,6,20) STO→ L1, then read the 20 rolls in STAT → EDIT. Use your table to turn each roll into its net, and find the average. Why does it differ from μX? What would 1,000 games do?
    Answer. Twenty-game averages vary a lot (their standard deviation is about 3.67/√20 ≈ $0.82), so most groups land somewhere between about −$1.50 and +$1.80. With 1,000 games the average settles near $0.17: the law of large numbers.

Poster 6 (spare) — Rigged coin?

DOK 3

Data

You make the data: 20 flips of a fair coin on the TI-84, where 1 = heads. randInt(0,1,20) STO→ L1, then read the flips down L1 in STAT → EDIT. (randInt is under MATH → PROB.)

The claim you are judging

"I flipped a coin 20 times and got 6 heads in a row. That can't happen with a fair coin. It's rigged."

Solve together first. Scratch paper and whiteboards, no poster yet.

  1. Design. Write the simulation in three sentences: how one flip is modeled, what one trial is, and what you record from each trial (the longest run of heads in the 20 flips).
    Answer. One flip: a random digit 0 or 1, 1 = heads, each equally likely. One trial: 20 such digits, randInt(0,1,20). Record: the longest string of consecutive 1s in the trial.
  2. Run 25 trials. Record the longest run each time. Draw a dotplot of the 25 values.
    Answer. Typical values 3 to 5; a 6 or more appears in roughly 3 of 25 trials.
  3. Estimate P(longest run is 6 or more) from your dotplot.
    Answer. (number of trials with a run of 6 or more)/25. Expect something near 0.12; anything from 0 to 0.24 is normal variation for 25 trials. If a group gets 0, pool the class's trials before the verdict.
  4. Verdict. First decide: how rare would 6 in a row have to be before you believed the coin was rigged? Then compare your estimate with that bar. Is 6 in a row convincing evidence?
    Answer. No. A fair coin gives a run of 6 or more heads in about 12% of sets of 20 flips, roughly one set in eight. That is not rare, so it is not convincing evidence of rigging. It does not prove the coin is fair, either.
  5. Your estimate is not the exact answer. Why not? What would 1,000 trials do? (Your teacher has the exact value.)
    Answer. A simulation gives a relative frequency, which varies from run to run; more trials make it settle closer to the exact value 0.1223 (law of large numbers). For reference: a run of 5 or more, 0.250; 7 or more, 0.058; 8 or more, 0.027.

A claim no card uses, so nothing is given away. Every poster has a title, the claim and the data, then the five things on its checklist.

1 · Title

"Healthy people hate flying"?

2 · The claim

"Healthy people hate flying: only 14 of them chose Fly."

3 · The data it rests on (from the Super Status table, 433 students)

Students who want to be Healthy: 51. Of those, chose Fly: 14. Students who chose Fly overall: 89 of 433.

4 · The probability the claim depends on

P(Fly | Healthy) = 14/51 = 27.5%   compared with   P(Fly) = 89/433 = 20.6%. The word "Healthy people" names the given group, so 51 is the denominator, not 433.

5 · The visual

27.5%
20.6%
Fly, among HealthyFly, everyone

6 · Verdict (the frame)

The claim says Healthy people hate flying. The number it really depends on is P(Fly | Healthy) = 27.5%, not the count 14. So the claim is not supported, because the Healthy group is small (51), and they chose Fly MORE often than students overall (27.5% vs 20.6%).

7 · Repair

"Students who want to be Healthy chose Fly more often than the class overall: 27.5% vs 20.6%."

Talk-track (3 minutes)

  1. "Read the claim. Which word names a group? Healthy people. That group's total goes in the denominator: 51, not 433."
  2. "Now compare: inside the group, 27.5%; everyone, 20.6%. The claim is backwards. That is the verdict, and the proof is the two numbers."
  3. "The repair is a true sentence with the percent in it. Your poster needs a title, the claim and the data, then the five things on your checklist. The checklist is the rubric."

Hint cards — tiered, hand out, never post

Tier 1 goes to a blank board at minute 28 on Tuesday. Tier 2 goes only to a group still stuck after tier 1, and only on the part they are stuck on. Frames come after thinking, not before.

Tier 1 (any card)

Find the word in the claim that names a group: given, of, among, who, people. Circle that group's TOTAL in the table. That total is your denominator. Now find the cell the claim is about and write the fraction.

Tier 2 · Card 1

Freeze Time row: 26 out of 101. Telepathy row: 19 out of 68. Which is bigger as a percent? Do all five rows the same way.

Tier 2 · Card 2

First sentence: you are inside the Famous COLUMN (15 people). Second sentence: inside the Fly ROW (89 people). Same cell, 5, on top of both.

Tier 2 · Card 3

Mutually exclusive asks: can both happen? (Look at the cell.) Independent asks: does knowing one CHANGE the chance of the other? Compare P(Famous) = 15/433 with P(Famous | Telepathy) = 0/68.

Tier 2 · Card 4

Draw two overlapping circles. Put 245 in the overlap FIRST. Grad-only = 693 − 245. Internet-only = 687 − 245. Neither = 2,500 minus everything inside the circles.

Tier 2 · Card 5

Net = winnings − 2. Rolls 2, 3, 4 and 5 all give the same net, so group them: probability 4/6. Mean = each net times its probability, added up.

Tier 2 · Card 6

MATH → PROB → randInt(0,1,20) STO→ L1 puts 20 flips in L1. Read down L1 in STAT → EDIT and count the longest string of 1s. Run it again for the next trial.

  1. False. Healthy and Telepathy are mutually exclusive (0 of 68), not independent: P(Healthy) = 51/433 = 11.8% but P(Healthy | Telepathy) = 0/68 = 0%. Knowing Telepathy changes the chance.

Debrief notes

  • The two persistent rules: the word after "given" names the group whose total is the denominator (posters 1, 2, 4); mutually exclusive is the opposite of unrelated (poster 3, the 10/29 promise).
  • Card 1 nuance: the newsletter's number is right and its reasoning is wrong. Accept "not supported" or "partly supported" when the sentence says the count is true and the percent does not stand out.
  • Card 5 nuance: fair means μX = 0, for both sides; the question is who an unfair game favors. The 20-game averages will scatter; put them on one class dotplot and the law of large numbers teaches itself.
  • Worksheet note (not for students): the 2.7 follow-along's union examples print Super Status numbers (265 + 29 − 15; 115/433 + 29/433) that do not match the printed table (Super Strength is 77, Freeze Time 101, Happy and Super Strength 43). Every number in this kit uses the printed table; the worksheet blanks need a fix in a later pass.
