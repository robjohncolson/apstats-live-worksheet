Unit 1 Poster — Data, Graph, Sentence

Five posters. Each shows one Unit 1 data set, one kind of display, and the answer to one DOK-3 question in a sentence or two. Together the five posters cover every Unit 1 display.

Printables (PDF): five group sheets (2 pages each, print one per group) · teacher key (answers, finished graphs, scoring grid) · example poster + talk-track (project this first). Two days are on the calendar: Period B Thu 10/8 + Fri 10/9, Period E Fri 10/9 + Wed 10/14.
The hook for students: "Every question on these cards is built from a mistake this class made over and over on Unit 1 worksheets and quizzes. Your group solves it together, then puts the data, the graph, and your answer on the poster so nobody in this room makes it again on the Progress Check."

Roles inside a trio

The whole group solves the DOK-3 question together first, on scratch paper, before anyone touches the poster. Then:

  • Data Keeper — writes the data table on the poster and does every computation shown (percents, five-number summary, fences, mean and median). Shows the arithmetic.
  • Grapher — draws the display(s): title, labeled axes with units, a scale that starts and ends sensibly, a key if two groups are shown.
  • Writer — writes the answer in one or two sentences, in context, with at least one number from the graph, and checks the must-have list before the walk.

In a pair, the Data Keeper also writes.

Poster 1 — Frequency Table + Bar Chart

Superpower survey · Lessons 1.2–1.4
Your poster must show: a frequency table AND a bar chart of the same data.

Data (printed in Lesson 1.3)

A random sample of 50 high school students was asked which superpower they would most like to have. Fly 9 Freeze time 15 Invisibility 7 Super strength 3 Telepathy 16

DOK-3 question — solve together before you build

A streaming service wants to make a show called Freeze Time. Their pitch says: "Freeze time is the superpower teens want most, and it's twice as popular as flying." A second writer says the show should be about telepathy instead.

(a) Build the frequency table and bar chart. (b) Decide which parts of the pitch the data support and which they don't, with numbers. (c) Write the one claim the service could honestly make about the two most popular powers. (d) Explain why this data gets a bar chart and not a dotplot or histogram: what are the individuals, what is the variable, and what type is it?

The mistakes this busts

On the Charleston reflection, 11 of 29 students defined "individual" correctly but never said who the individuals were, and 10 couldn't name a variable from the sheet. Eight treated grade level and six treated zip code as quantitative because they're written with digits. And the most-flagged line in the whole class: a number stated with no variable, no units, no question attached (13 students on one Flint reflection alone).

Poster must-haves

  • Frequency table with a total row that adds to 50
  • Bar chart: title, both axes labeled, bars separated, count scale that fits the tallest bar
  • Individuals, variable, and variable type (categorical) named in a sentence
  • The pitch judged claim by claim with numbers: "most" (telepathy 16 beats freeze time 15), "twice as popular" (15 vs 9)
  • One honest claim in context with a number, such as what fraction chose telepathy or freeze time
Data KeeperThe table with totals and the "most" / "twice" arithmetic.
GrapherThe bar chart with title, axes, scale.
WriterIndividuals / variable / type sentence and the honest-claim sentence.

Poster 2 — Relative Frequency Table + Relative Frequency Bar Chart

PERSISTENT in Period B
Spirit day survey · Lesson 1.4
Your poster must show: a relative frequency table for both grades AND a side-by-side relative frequency bar chart with a key.

Data (printed in Lesson 1.4)

A teacher surveyed two grades about their favorite spirit day theme. Theme 9th grade (30 students) 10th grade (20 students) Pajama Day 12 5 Jersey Day 6 8 Twin Day 9 3 Costume Day 3 4

DOK-3 question — solve together before you build

Student council voted for Jersey Day as the schoolwide theme. Their reasoning: "Jersey Day got the most 10th-grade votes, and 9th grade was close anyway: 6 versus 8."

(a) Convert every count to a relative frequency (percent) within its grade. (b) Draw the side-by-side relative frequency bar chart. (c) Decide which theme each grade actually prefers and whether "9th grade was close" is true once you compare percents. (d) The two grades disagree. Recommend one schoolwide theme and defend it with percents, not counts. Then explain why a chart of raw counts would have misled the council.

The mistake this busts

The only misconception the tracker calls persistent in Period B: the same students, two lessons, days apart. Six students could not explain why relative frequencies beat counts when the groups are 30 and 20; five couldn't turn 4 of 40 into 10%; the same thing hit the 80-males vs 125-females comparison. On quiz U1-L4-Q02, students picked claims about "the greatest number" from a chart that only showed relative frequencies.

Poster must-haves

  • Relative frequency table: eight percents, each column summing to 100%, arithmetic shown for at least two
  • Side-by-side relative frequency bar chart: title, percent axis, key for the two grades
  • Each grade's actual favorite named with its percent (Pajama 40% vs Jersey 40%)
  • "6 vs 8 is close" rejected with percents (20% vs 40%)
  • A schoolwide recommendation defended with percents, plus one sentence on why counts mislead when groups differ in size
Data KeeperEight percents, column totals, the 20% vs 40% comparison.
GrapherThe side-by-side chart with key and percent scale.
WriterEach grade's favorite, the recommendation, the "why counts mislead" sentence.

Poster 3 — Dotplot

Late-bus wait · Lessons 1.5–1.7
Your poster must show: a dotplot with every value as a dot, on a number line with a scale.

Data (printed in Lesson 1.6)

A school recorded the number of minutes 11 students waited for a late bus after school. 1, 2, 3, 3, 4, 4, 4, 5, 5, 6, 18

DOK-3 question — solve together before you build

The transportation office reports: "Students wait an average of 5 minutes for the late bus, so there is no problem."

(a) Draw the dotplot. (b) Describe the distribution fully: shape, center, variability, and unusual features, in context. (c) Is 18 an outlier? Show the 1.5 × IQR rule. (d) The mean is exactly 5.0 and the median is 4. Explain, using the dotplot, why the office's "average of 5" sentence hides the actual problem, and rewrite their sentence so it tells the truth in one or two lines.

The mistakes this busts

Six of 29 students never mentioned unusual features (gaps, clusters, outliers) when asked to describe a distribution, and four described shape but skipped variability. Five said the variable in a study was "the students" instead of the measurement. Many picked the mean as "typical" for skewed data; in Period E that tag is persistent (8 students, 12 answers).

Poster must-haves

  • Dotplot: 11 dots, number line from 0 to 20 with even tick marks, axis label with units, title
  • Shape, center, variability, and unusual features each in its own labeled sentence, in context (minutes waiting)
  • Outlier check shown: Q1 3, Q3 5, IQR 2, fence 5 + 3 = 8, so 18 is an outlier
  • Mean 5.0 vs median 4 explained from the dotplot: one dot dragged the mean
  • The office's sentence rewritten honestly, with a number
Data KeeperFive-number summary, IQR, fence, mean.
GrapherThe dotplot with a clean scale.
WriterThe SOCS sentences and the honest rewrite.

Poster 4 — Stem-and-Leaf Plot + Histogram

PERSISTENT in Period E
Resting heart rate · Lessons 1.2, 1.5, 1.7, 1.8
Your poster must show: a stem-and-leaf plot AND a histogram (bins of 5 bpm) of the same 25 values, side by side.

Data (Lesson 1.2 spring-sport context)

Resting heart rate, in beats per minute, for 25 students who play a spring sport (already in order): 42, 46, 50, 54, 57, 58, 59, 60, 60, 61, 62, 62, 62, 63, 64, 64, 65, 65, 66, 66, 67, 68, 68, 69, 70

DOK-3 question — solve together before you build

The coach says: "Resting heart rate is skewed, so the mean is pulled up. I'll use the mean as the typical athlete, and anyone below 50 bpm has to see the trainer."

(a) Make the stem-and-leaf plot and the histogram. (b) Describe the shape. Which direction is the tail? Which way does that push the mean relative to the median? Check with the actual mean and median. (c) Which measure of center should the coach report as "typical," and why? (d) Use the 1.5 × IQR rule to decide which athletes are outliers. Does the coach's "below 50" rule agree with the statistics? (e) The trainer wants to see individual athletes' numbers. Which of your two displays do you hand her, and what does the other one hide?

The mistakes this busts

The tracker's largest persistent flag: in Period E, 8 students were tagged "treats the mean as resistant to outliers" across 12 answers. On quiz U1-L8-Q03 most of the class chose "mean greater than median because skewed right" for a histogram that was skewed left. Five never said a histogram bins values, and four missed that only dotplots and stem-and-leaf plots show every value.

Poster must-haves

  • Stem-and-leaf plot with stems 4–7, leaves in order, and a key (e.g. 6 | 2 = 62 bpm)
  • Histogram: bins 40–44 through 70–74, bars touching, labeled axes with units, title
  • Shape named with the tail direction (skewed left), and mean 61.1 vs median 62 shown to confirm which way the tail pulls
  • Outlier check shown: Q1 58.5, Q3 66, IQR 7.5, low fence 47.25, so 42 and 46 are outliers and 50 is not; coach's rule compared
  • A sentence choosing the center to report and a sentence saying which display shows every athlete and what the histogram hides
Data KeeperMean (sum 1528 ÷ 25), median, quartiles, fences.
GrapherBoth displays, side by side, same data.
WriterShape + direction sentence, the center choice, the trainer sentence.

Poster 5 — Parallel Box Plots

Study halls · Lessons 1.8, 1.9
Your poster must show: two box plots on one shared number line, outlier plotted as a separate point.

Data (printed in Lesson 1.9)

Nightly homework time, in minutes, for students in two study halls. Study Hall A: minimum 30 Q1 45 median 60 Q3 90 largest non-outlier 110 one high outlier at 150 Study Hall B: minimum 45 Q1 54 median 60 Q3 66 maximum 75 no outliers

DOK-3 question — solve together before you build

The principal says: "Both study halls have a median of 60 minutes, so homework time is the same in both." A parent asks: "What percent of Study Hall A students spent more than 90 minutes?"

(a) Draw both box plots on one number line. (b) Compare the two distributions in shape, center, variability, and unusual features, with a comparative word in every sentence. (c) Answer the parent, and explain how a box plot lets you say that without seeing the data. (d) Is the principal right? Use at least two features besides the median. (e) A student wants the study hall where homework time is most predictable. Which one, and which box-plot feature proves it?

The mistakes this busts

On the salary comparison, six students missed the outliers, five never compared the medians, four skipped variability, and almost nobody used comparative words. On quiz U1-L8-Q02 most of the class read a box plot whose median sat at the cutoff and said 75% passed; the answer was 50%. On U1-L9-Q01 we said the medians matched when it was the IQRs. Three students gave the range when asked for the IQR.

Poster must-haves

  • Two box plots on one shared scale (30 to 150), labeled A and B, axis label with units, 150 plotted as a separate point
  • Four comparison sentences (shape, center, variability, unusual), each with a comparative word, in context
  • Parent answered: about 25% of A spent more than 90 minutes, with the "each piece is 25%" rule written on the plot
  • Principal judged with numbers: IQR 45 vs 12, range 120 vs 30, outlier vs none
  • Predictability choice (B) tied to the narrower box and shorter whiskers
Data KeeperIQRs, ranges, the 25% labels.
GrapherBoth plots on one scale, outlier as a point.
WriterFour comparative sentences, the parent's answer, the choice.

Answer notes

  • 1: Telepathy 16 is the most, not freeze time 15. 15 vs 9 is not twice. Honest claim: 31 of 50 (62%) chose telepathy or freeze time. Individuals = the 50 students; variable = preferred superpower, categorical.
  • 2: 9th: Pajama 40, Jersey 20, Twin 30, Costume 10. 10th: Pajama 25, Jersey 40, Twin 15, Costume 20. 9th prefers Pajama, 10th prefers Jersey; 20% vs 40% is not close. Any defended pick is fine; Pajama (40 / 25) is the only theme at 25% or better in both grades.
  • 3: Skewed right, one peak near 4, typical 4 minutes, spread 1 to 18, gap 6 to 18, 18 is an outlier (fence 8). Mean 5.0, median 4. Honest version: most students wait about 4 minutes, but one waited 18.
  • 4: Skewed left (tail toward low values). Mean 61.1 < median 62: the coach has the direction backwards. Report the median. Q1 58.5, Q3 66, IQR 7.5, low fence 47.25: 42 and 46 are outliers, 50 is not, so "below 50" happens to match the statistics here but was not derived from them. Hand the trainer the stem-and-leaf plot; the histogram hides individual values.
  • 5: A: IQR 45, range 120, one outlier. B: IQR 12, range 30, none. Same median, very different variability, so the principal is wrong. Parent: about 25% of A spent over 90 minutes (Q3 = 90). Predictable: B.

Debrief prompts

  • Stand by your period's persistent poster (2 in B, 4 in E). "This one came back after we taught it. What on this poster would have stopped it the second time?"
  • "Every poster has the same hidden rule in its answer sentence. What is it?" (A number means nothing without its variable, units, and question.)
  • Three students read their "which mistake got you" line aloud.

After the walk the misconception tracker keeps counting. If a cluster drops off the dashboard's "most frequent" list over the next two weeks, the poster worked. If it stays, the weekly DOK sheet picks it up again.
