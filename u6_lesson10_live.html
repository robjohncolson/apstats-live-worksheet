Topic 6.10: Setting Up a Test for the Difference of Two Population Proportion

Null and Alternative Hypotheses, Test Selection, and Condition Checks
Video Follow-Along Worksheet
Learning Objectives:
  • VAR-6.H: Identify the null and alternative hypotheses for a difference of two population proportions.
  • VAR-6.I: Identify an appropriate testing method for the difference of two population proportions.
  • VAR-6.J: Verify the conditions for making statistical inferences when testing a difference of two population proportions.
Essential Knowledge:
  • For tests of a difference in proportions, the null states equality: p1 = p2 or p1 - p2 = 0.
  • The alternative uses a strict inequality: greater than, less than, or not equal to.
  • The correct procedure is a two-sample z-test for a difference in population proportions.
  • Condition checks include independence, the 10% condition for random samples, and large expected counts using the pooled proportion.

Key Vocabulary

Null Hypothesis The no-difference claim, usually H0: p1 - p2 = 0.
Alternative Hypothesis The claim being tested, written with a strict inequality (>, <, or ≠).
One-Sided Alternative A directional claim such as p1 > p2 or p1 < p2.
Two-Sided Alternative A non-directional claim: p1 ≠ p2.
Two-Sample z-Test The significance test used for a difference between two population proportions.
Pooled Proportion The combined success rate, p̂c = (x1 + x2)/(n1 + n2).
10% Condition For random samples without replacement, each sample size is at most 10% of its population.
Expected Counts For shape, check n1p̂c, n1(1-p̂c), n2p̂c, n2(1-p̂c) are all at least 10.

Key Takeaways

[Summary]
  1. State hypotheses with parameters: null uses equality, alternative uses a strict inequality.
  2. Choose the alternative form (>, <, or ≠) from the research question before looking at results.
  3. Use a two-sample z-test for a difference in population proportions.
  4. Check independence and expected counts with the pooled proportion before running the test.
