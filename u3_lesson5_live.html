Topic 3.5: Introduction to Experimental Design

How experiments, randomization, and control help establish causation
Video Follow-Along Worksheet
Learning Objectives:
  • Identify the components of an experiment, including experimental units, treatments, factors, and response variables.
  • Explain why observational studies cannot establish causation when confounding variables may be present.
  • Describe the four elements of a well-designed experiment: comparison, random assignment, replication, and control.
  • Compare completely randomized designs, randomized block designs, and matched pairs designs.
  • Explain the roles of control groups, placebos, and blinding in experiments.
Essential Knowledge:
  • Experimental units are the individuals assigned treatments; when they are people, they may be called participants or subjects.
  • An explanatory variable, or factor, is intentionally manipulated in an experiment, and its levels are treatments.
  • A response variable is the measured outcome after treatments are applied.
  • A confounding variable is related to the explanatory variable and influences the response variable, possibly creating a false perception of association.
  • A well-designed experiment includes comparison, random assignment, replication, and control of potential confounding variables.
  • Completely randomized designs assign treatments at random to all units, while randomized block designs randomize within similar blocks.
  • Placebos, control groups, and blinding help reduce bias and isolate treatment effects.

Key Vocabulary

ExperimentA study in which treatments are intentionally imposed on experimental units.
Experimental UnitAn individual or object that receives a treatment in an experiment.
Participant / SubjectA human experimental unit.
FactorThe explanatory variable that is intentionally manipulated in an experiment.
TreatmentA level or combination of levels of the explanatory variable.
Response VariableThe outcome measured after treatments are administered.
Confounding VariableA variable related to the explanatory variable that also influences the response variable.
ComparisonUsing at least two treatment groups so responses can be compared.
Random AssignmentAssigning treatments to experimental units by chance.
ReplicationUsing enough experimental units in each treatment group.
ControlHolding other variables as constant as possible to reduce confounding.
Control GroupA group that does not receive the treatment of interest or receives an inactive treatment.
Completely Randomized DesignA design in which all treatments are assigned completely at random to all units.
Randomized Block DesignA design in which units are first grouped into similar blocks, then randomized within each block.
PlaceboA fake treatment similar to the treatment being tested.
Placebo EffectA response by experimental units to a placebo.
Double-BlindNeither the subjects nor the interacting researchers know which treatment each subject receives.
Matched Pairs DesignA special case of a block design that uses pairs or has each subject receive both treatments.

Design Vocabulary from Video 3 [2:20 – 9:30]
  • Completely randomized design: all units are randomly assigned to treatments.
  • Randomized block design: group similar units into blocks, then randomize treatments within each block.
  • Placebo: a fake treatment used to help detect whether the treatment effect is real.
  • Single-blind: either the subjects or the interacting researchers do not know the treatments.
  • Double-blind: neither the subjects nor the interacting researchers know the treatments.
  • Matched pairs design: a special case of a block design that uses pairs or gives each subject both treatments.
