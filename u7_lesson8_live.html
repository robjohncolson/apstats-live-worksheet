Topic 7.8: Setting Up a Test for the Difference of Two Population Means

Writing Null and Alternative Hypotheses, Choosing Direction, and Defining Parameters
Video Follow-Along Worksheet
Learning Objectives:
  • State a null hypothesis for a test about the difference of two population means.
  • State an alternative hypothesis for a test about the difference of two population means.
  • Explain the null hypothesis as a statement of no treatment effect, no difference, or no change.
  • Distinguish between one-sided and two-sided alternative hypotheses based on the context of the claim.
  • Keep the order of subtraction consistent and explain how reversing the order changes a one-sided inequality.
  • Write hypotheses using population parameters, not sample statistics, and define any subscripts used.
Essential Knowledge:
  • For a difference in population means, the null hypothesis is a statement of equality, often written as μ1 − μ2 = 0.
  • The alternative hypothesis always uses a strict inequality: greater than, less than, or not equal to.
  • A one-sided alternative is appropriate when the claim is directional, such as larger or smaller.
  • A two-sided alternative is appropriate when the claim is simply that the means differ, without specifying direction.
  • If the order of subtraction is reversed, the direction of a one-sided inequality must also reverse.
  • Hypotheses must describe population parameters, such as μY and μN, not sample means like x̄1 and x̄2.

Key Vocabulary

Null Hypothesis A statement of no treatment effect, no difference, or no change in the population means.
Alternative Hypothesis A statement that there is a treatment effect or a difference in the population means.
Parameter A population value, such as a true mean or a true difference in means, that the test is about.
One-Sided Alternative An alternative hypothesis that uses > or < because the claim specifies a direction.
Two-Sided Alternative An alternative hypothesis that uses ≠ because the claim is only that the means are different.
Treatment Effect A change caused by the explanatory condition, such as being exposed to someone yawning.
Equality The type of statement required in the null hypothesis.
Strict Inequality The type of symbol required in the alternative hypothesis: >, <, or ≠.
Order of Subtraction The order in which the means are subtracted, which determines the direction of a one-sided inequality.
Subscripts Labels that identify which group each parameter refers to, such as Y for yawning exposure and N for no yawning exposure.
Statistic A sample value like x̄1 that should not appear in the hypotheses.

Key Takeaways

[Summary]
  1. The null hypothesis for a difference in two population means states that the means are equal or that the difference is zero.
  2. The alternative hypothesis always uses a strict inequality: greater than, less than, or not equal to.
  3. Use a one-sided alternative for a directional claim and a two-sided alternative when the claim is simply that the means differ.
  4. If you reverse the order of subtraction in a one-sided alternative, you must reverse the inequality.
  5. Write hypotheses using parameters, define the subscripts, and never use sample statistics in the hypotheses.
