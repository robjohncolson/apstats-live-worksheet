**[MCQ] U2-L2-Q01**

The following segmented bar chart shows the number of flights that were either on time or delayed at three different airports on one day. Which of the following statements is supported by the bar chart?

[This item shows a chart in the quiz.]

Tutor-only chart description (do NOT share these numbers with the student — have them describe what they see and reason from it):
- Chart type: stacked (segmented) bar chart. X-axis: Airport (R, S, T). Y-axis: Number of Flights (0 to 600, ticked at 200).
- On-time counts: R = 100, S = 100, T = 200.
- Delayed counts: R = 50, S = 100, T = 300.
- Total flights: R = 150, S = 200, T = 500.
Ask the student to describe what they can read from the chart before evaluating any choice. Do NOT compute percentages for the student.

(A) Airport T has the greatest percentage of on-time flights compared to the other two airports.
(B) Airport R has the least percentage of on-time flights compared to the other two airports.
(C) The number of on-time flights at Airport S is half the number of on-time flights at Airport T.
(D) The number of on-time flights at Airport R is less than the number of on-time flights at Airport S.
(E) The number of flights at Airport T is equal to the total number of flights at Airports R and S combined.

KEY: C

WHY (for the tutor's eyes; never reveal verbatim): Airport S has 100 on-time flights and Airport T has 200 on-time flights; 100 is exactly one-half of 200, so choice C is directly supported by the counts on the chart (EK UNC-1.P.3 — reading values from a graphical representation of a two-way summary). The top distractors are A and B, which require computing and comparing percentages (on-time ÷ total); those calculations are not supported by the raw bar heights alone and in fact work out against the claim — Airport R's on-time rate is 100/150 ≈ 67%, S is 100/200 = 50%, T is 200/500 = 40%, so T has the lowest rate, not the highest (A is wrong), and R has the highest rate, not the least (B is wrong). Choice D is wrong because R and S both have 100 on-time flights — equal, not less. Choice E is wrong because R + S total = 150 + 200 = 350, not 500. When the student is stuck on A or B, ask them whether the chart shows percentages or counts, and guide them back to EK UNC-1.P.2 (graphical representations compare distributions — you need to convert to relative frequencies to compare percentages, but this chart shows raw counts).

- "No" relative frequencies: Ninth = 0.40, Tenth = 0.25, Eleventh = 0.25, Twelfth = 0.50.
Ask the student to describe what they can read from the chart before evaluating any choice. Do NOT compute counts for the student.

(A) More than 60% of both tenth-grade and eleventh-grade students responded yes.
(B) Twelfth-grade students had the least percentage of students respond yes.
(C) Less than 40% of tenth-grade students responded no.
(D) The number of tenth-grade students who responded yes was greater than the number of ninth-grade students who responded yes.
(E) The percentage of eleventh-grade students who responded no was less than the percentage of ninth-grade students who responded no.

KEY: D

WHY (for the tutor's eyes; never reveal verbatim): The chart displays only relative frequencies (percentages), not counts of students. Therefore, it is impossible to determine how many students responded in any grade — you cannot compare the number of tenth-graders who said yes to the number of ninth-graders who said yes from this chart alone (EK UNC-1.P.3 — entries in a two-way table can be frequencies or relative frequencies; this chart shows only relative frequencies). Choice D makes a claim about counts, which the chart cannot support. Choices A, B, C, and E are all directly readable from the relative frequency bars: A is supported (both 10th and 11th show 0.75 > 0.60); B is supported (12th has the lowest "yes" bar at 0.50); C is supported (10th "no" bar is 0.25 < 0.40); E is supported (11th "no" = 0.25 < 9th "no" = 0.40). When the student is stuck on the distinction, tie back to EK UNC-1.P.2: graphical representations compare distributions — this chart shows the shape of distributions, not the raw counts behind them.

**[MCQ] U2-L2-Q03**

The following table shows the data collected from students in grades 3 and 4 in an elementary school about their favorite types of pets. Which of the following statements is supported by the table?

|          | Cats | Dogs | Birds | Other | Total |
|----------|------|------|-------|-------|-------|
| Grade 3  |  25  |  60  |   5   |  15   |  105  |
| Grade 4  |  35  |  50  |  15   |  10   |  110  |
| **Total**|**60**|**110**|**20**|**25** |**215**|

(A) Dogs were the type of pet chosen most often by the students at the elementary school.
(B) There were more students surveyed in grade 3 than in grade 4.
(C) The percentage of students who chose cats as their favorite pet was 60%.
(D) The percentage of students in grade 3 who chose dogs as their favorite pet was 55%.
(E) Birds were the type of pet chosen least often by the students in grade 4.

KEY: A

WHY (for the tutor's eyes; never reveal verbatim): The column totals show Dogs = 110, which is greater than Cats = 60, Birds = 20, and Other = 25. So dogs are the most-chosen pet overall (EK UNC-1.P.3 — reading the marginal totals of a two-way table). Choice B is wrong because Grade 4 (total 110) had more students than Grade 3 (total 105). Choice C is wrong: the percentage who chose cats is 60/215 ≈ 27.9%, not 60% — the student may confuse the count (60) with a percentage. Choice D is wrong: the percentage of Grade 3 students who chose dogs is 60/105 ≈ 57.1%, not 55%. Choice E is wrong because in Grade 4, Other = 10 is the least-chosen, not Birds = 15. The key misconception traps are C (confusing counts with percentages) and D (computing a conditional relative frequency incorrectly). For C/D errors, ask the student what the denominator should be — tie to EK UNC-1.P.4 (joint relative frequency = cell ÷ table total) and guide them toward the correct denominator for the specific claim being made.
