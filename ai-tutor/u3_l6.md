(Supporting background from Topic 3.5, required to reason about design choice:)
- EK VAR-3.C.1 — In a completely randomized design, treatments are assigned to experimental units completely at random. Random assignment tends to balance the effects of uncontrolled (confounding) variables so that differences in responses can be attributed to the treatments.
- EK VAR-3.C.7 — For randomized complete block designs, treatments are assigned completely at random within each block.
- EK VAR-3.C.8 — Blocking ensures that at the beginning of the experiment the units within each block are similar to each other with respect to at least one blocking variable. A randomized block design helps to separate natural variability from differences due to the blocking variable.

[MCQ] U3-L6-Q01

The quality-control manager of a large factory is concerned about the number of defective items produced by workers. Thirty workers at the factory agree to participate in a study of three different incentive plans to help reduce the number of defective items produced. The plans will be randomly assigned to the workers so that 10 workers received each plan. The reduction in the number of defective items produced by each worker will be recorded two weeks after the plans are implemented.

Which of the following best describes why a completely randomized design is an appropriate design to use in this situation?

(A) There is no blocking variable, and incentive plans will be randomly assigned to the workers.
(B) There is no blocking variable, and the workers were selected at random.
(C) Each incentive plan is a block, and a completely randomized design is not blocked.
(D) Each plan will be randomly assigned to 10 pairs of workers who share a similar characteristic.
(E) The number of workers participating in the study was greater than or equal to 30.

KEY: A

WHY (for the tutor's eyes; never reveal verbatim): A completely randomized design is appropriate because there is no blocking variable identified in the study — no characteristic of the workers (e.g., experience level, shift) is mentioned that would warrant grouping. The treatments (incentive plans) are randomly assigned to the 30 workers, which is the defining feature of a completely randomized design (EK VAR-3.C.1) and satisfies LO VAR-3.D. Choice B is the top distractor: the workers were NOT selected at random from some larger population — they agreed to participate — so "randomly selected" is incorrect; the relevant fact is random assignment of treatments, not random selection of participants. Choice C confuses plans with blocks; in a CRD, plans are the treatments, not the blocking variable. Choice D describes a paired/blocked structure that does not exist here. Choice E is irrelevant — sample size alone does not justify design choice.

[MCQ] U3-L6-Q02

A market research firm is studying the effects of price and type of packaging on sales of a particular product. Twenty-seven stores with shoppers of similar characteristics will be used in the study. The nine combinations of three price levels and three packaging types are the treatments of interest. Total sales of the product over a seven-week period will be recorded. Which of the following describes the best design to use for the study?

(A) A completely randomized design. Randomly assign the nine combinations of price level and packaging type so that three stores use each combination.
(B) A completely randomized design. Randomly assign the three price levels so that nine stores use each price level.
(C) A completely randomized design. Randomly assign the three packaging types so that nine stores use each type of packaging.
(D) A randomized block design. Use packaging as a block. Randomly assign the nine combinations of three price levels and three packaging types so that three stores use each combination.
(E) A randomized block design. Use packaging as a block. Randomly assign nine stores to each packaging type, then randomly assign price level to three stores within each block.

KEY: A

WHY (for the tutor's eyes; never reveal verbatim): Because all 27 stores have shoppers of similar characteristics, there is no identified blocking variable that needs to be controlled — a completely randomized design is appropriate (EK VAR-3.D.1, EK VAR-3.C.1). The study is examining the combined effects of both price AND packaging type; the treatments are therefore the 9 combinations (3 × 3 = 9), not each factor alone. With 27 stores and 9 combinations, 3 stores receive each combination — this is choice A. Choices B and C are wrong because they test only one factor at a time, discarding information about the other factor and its interaction. Choices D and E incorrectly invoke a randomized block design: blocking is used to reduce variability due to a nuisance variable, but the problem states stores are already similar — there is no blocking variable. Students often pick D or E because they see "packaging" mentioned and assume it should be a block; the key insight is that blocking is needed only when experimental units differ on some variable that could confound results (LO VAR-3.D).

[MCQ] U3-L6-Q03

A researcher is studying the effect of genetically modified (GM) and nongenetically modified (nGM) corn on the weight gain of lambs. The sex and genetics of the lambs can affect their weight gain. Five sets of male twin lambs and five sets of female twin lambs—for a total of twenty lambs—are available for the study. The lambs will be randomly assigned to a diet of either GM or nGM diet of corn. Weight gain will be recorded for each lamb after five weeks on the diet. Which of the following designs would be best to use in the study?

(A) A completely randomized design. Randomly assign ten lambs to the GM diet and ten lambs to the nGM diet.
(B) A stratified randomized design. Divide the lambs into males and females. Within each group, randomly assign the GM diet to one half and the nGM diet to the other half.
(C) A randomized block design. Randomly assign ten lambs to the GM diet and ten lambs to the nGM diet.
(D) A randomized block design. Randomly assign the GM diet to the male lambs and the nGM diet to the female lambs.
(E) A matched pairs design. For each set of twins, randomly assign one twin to the GM diet and the other twin to the nGM diet.

KEY: E

WHY (for the tutor's eyes; never reveal verbatim): A matched pairs design is the best choice because twin lambs are naturally matched — they share the same sex and similar genetics, both of which the problem explicitly identifies as variables that can affect weight gain (EK VAR-3.C.9). By randomly assigning one twin of each pair to each diet, this design controls for both sex and genetic variation simultaneously, providing the most precise comparison of diet effects while minimizing confounding (EK VAR-3.D.1, LO VAR-3.D). Choice A (CRD) ignores the available pairing information and would allow the known confounders (sex, genetics) to create noise. Choice B misuses the term "stratified randomized" (a sampling term, not an experimental design term) and does not capitalize on the twin pairing. Choice C names a randomized block design but then describes random assignment without blocking — the description is identical to a CRD, not a blocked design. Choice D is particularly dangerous: it assigns ALL males to one diet and ALL females to the other, which completely confounds sex with diet — any observed difference cannot be attributed to diet alone. The key insight students must articulate: twins are natural matched pairs (EK VAR-3.C.9), and within each pair random assignment to GM vs. nGM isolates the diet effect from genetic/sex confounding.
