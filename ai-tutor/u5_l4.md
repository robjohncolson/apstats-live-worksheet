**[MCQ] U5-L4-Q01**

A certain statistic \(\hat{d}\) is being used to estimate a population parameter \(D\). The expected value of \(\hat{d}\) is not equal to \(D\). What property does \(\hat{d}\) exhibit?

(A) The sampling distribution of \(\hat{d}\) is normal.
(B) The sampling distribution of \(\hat{d}\) is binomial.
(C) The sampling distribution of \(\hat{d}\) is uniform.
(D) \(\hat{d}\) is unbiased.
(E) \(\hat{d}\) is biased.

KEY: E

WHY (for the tutor's eyes; never reveal verbatim): This item directly tests LO UNC-3.I and EK UNC-3.I.1. A biased estimator is one whose expected value (the long-run average of its sampling distribution) is not equal to the population parameter it estimates. Since the expected value of \(\hat{d}\) is not equal to \(D\), the definition of bias is satisfied and E is correct. Options A, B, and C describe the shape of a sampling distribution, which is irrelevant to bias — shape does not determine whether an estimator is biased or unbiased. Option D is the direct opposite of the truth. The top distractor is D — students who confuse the definition of unbiased with the idea that any estimator "tries" to approximate the parameter may choose it.

**[MCQ] U5-L4-Q02**

Which of the following conditions will create a biased estimator of a population parameter?

(A) The sampling distribution of the estimator is skewed to the left.
(B) The sampling distribution of the estimator is skewed to the right.
(C) The sampling distribution of the estimator is not the same shape as the distribution of the population parameter.
(D) The expected value of the estimator is not equal to the population parameter.
(E) The variability of the sampling distribution of the estimator is not equal to the variability of the population parameter.

KEY: D

WHY (for the tutor's eyes; never reveal verbatim): This item tests LO UNC-3.I and EK UNC-3.I.1 by asking students to identify the defining condition of a biased estimator. Bias is defined solely by the relationship between the expected value of the estimator and the population parameter: if the expected value of the estimator is not equal to the parameter, the estimator is biased. Options A and B address the shape (skewness) of the sampling distribution — irrelevant to bias. Option C addresses shape mismatch between the estimator's distribution and the population distribution — also irrelevant. Option E addresses variability (spread), which is a separate property from bias. The top distractor is E — students often conflate variability with bias, treating a wide or unequal-spread sampling distribution as a source of "bias" rather than understanding that bias is strictly about where the distribution is centered.

**[MCQ] U5-L4-Q03**

Samples G and H were selected from the same population of quantitative data and the mean of each sample was determined. The mean of sample G is equal to the mean of the population. Which of the following statements must be true?

I. The mean of sample H must also be equal to the population mean.
II. The mean of sample G, \(\bar{x}_G\), is a point estimator for the mean of the population.
III. The mean of sample H, \(\bar{x}_H\), is a point estimator for the mean of the population.

(A) I only
(B) II only
(C) III only
(D) I and II
(E) II and III

KEY: E

WHY (for the tutor's eyes; never reveal verbatim): This item tests both LO UNC-3.J / EK UNC-3.J.2 (any sample mean is a point estimator of the corresponding population mean) and LO UNC-3.I / EK UNC-3.I.1 (an unbiased estimator equals the parameter on average, not in every individual sample). Statement II is true: any sample mean, including \(\bar{x}_G\), serves as a point estimator of the population mean by definition (EK UNC-3.J.2). Statement III is true for the same reason: \(\bar{x}_H\) is also a point estimator regardless of what value it takes. Statement I is false: sample means vary from sample to sample due to sampling variability (EK UNC-3.J.1). The fact that sample G happened to match the population mean does not force sample H to do the same — that would only be guaranteed if there were no sampling variability at all. The top distractor is D — students may think that if one sample mean equals the parameter, a second sample from the same population must also equal it.

**[MCQ] U5-L4-Q05**

A certain statistic will be used as an unbiased estimator of a parameter. Let \(J\) represent the sampling distribution of the estimator for samples of size 40, and let \(K\) represent the sampling distribution of the estimator for samples of size 100. Which of the following must be true about \(J\) and \(K\)?

(A) The expected value of \(J\) will be equal to the expected value of \(K\), and the variability of \(J\) will be equal to the variability of \(K\).
(B) The expected value of \(J\) will be greater than the expected value of \(K\), and the variability of \(J\) will be greater than the variability of \(K\).
(C) The expected value of \(J\) will be greater than the expected value of \(K\), and the variability of \(J\) will be less than the variability of \(K\).
(D) The expected values of \(J\) and \(K\) will be equal, and the variability of \(J\) will equal the variability of \(K\).
(E) The expected values of \(J\) and \(K\) will be equal, and the variability of \(J\) will be greater than the variability of \(K\).

KEY: E

WHY (for the tutor's eyes; never reveal verbatim): This item tests both EK UNC-3.I.1 (unbiased estimators have expected values equal to the parameter) and EK UNC-3.J.1 (estimators exhibit variability modeled by probability — and larger samples reduce that variability). Because the estimator is stated to be unbiased, both \(J\) and \(K\) have expected values equal to the population parameter — the expected values are equal regardless of sample size (EK UNC-3.I.1). However, larger samples produce sampling distributions with less spread: since \(J\) uses n=40 and \(K\) uses n=100, the variability of \(J\) will be greater than the variability of \(K\). Options B and C incorrectly claim the expected values differ — an unbiased estimator's expected value is the parameter for any sample size. Option A and D correctly equate the expected values but incorrectly equate the variabilities. The top distractor is A or D — students who correctly identify equal expected values may not recall that variability decreases with larger samples.

**[MCQ] U5-L4-Q06**

The statistic \(\bar{x}\) is used as an estimator for which of the following?

(A) \(n\)
(B) \(s\)
(C) \(\sigma\)
(D) \(\mu\)
(E) \(z\)

KEY: D

WHY (for the tutor's eyes; never reveal verbatim): This item tests EK UNC-3.J.2 directly: a sample statistic is a point estimator of the corresponding population parameter. \(\bar{x}\) is the sample mean; it estimates \(\mu\), the population mean. Option A (\(n\)) is the sample size, not a parameter estimated by \(\bar{x}\). Option B (\(s\)) is the sample standard deviation — a statistic itself, not a parameter. Option C (\(\sigma\)) is the population standard deviation, which is estimated by \(s\), not \(\bar{x}\). Option E (\(z\)) is a standardized score, not a population parameter. The top distractor is C — students may loosely associate \(\bar{x}\) with "something about the population" and pick the population standard deviation without distinguishing between the mean and the standard deviation as separate parameters.
