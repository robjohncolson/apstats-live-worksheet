- **LO VAR-6.H** — Identify the null and alternative hypotheses for a difference of two population proportions. [Skill 1.F]
  - **EK VAR-6.H.1** — For a two-sample test for a difference of two proportions, the null hypothesis specifies a value of 0 for the difference in population proportions, indicating no difference or effect.
  - **EK VAR-6.H.2** — The null hypothesis for a difference in proportions is: H₀: p₁ = p₂, or H₀: p₁ − p₂ = 0.
  - **EK VAR-6.H.3** — A one-sided alternative hypothesis for a difference in proportions is Hₐ: p₁ < p₂ or Hₐ: p₁ > p₂. A two-sided alternative hypothesis is Hₐ: p₁ ≠ p₂.
- **LO VAR-6.I** — Identify an appropriate testing method for the difference of two population proportions. [Skill 1.E]

- **LO VAR-6.J** — Verify the conditions for making statistical inferences when testing a difference of two population proportions. [Skill 4.C]
  - **EK VAR-6.J.1** — To make statistical inferences when testing a difference between population proportions, check for independence and approximately normal sampling distribution:
    - Independence: data collected using two independent, random samples or a randomized experiment; when sampling without replacement, check n₁ ≤ 10%N₁ and n₂ ≤ 10%N₂.

[MCQ] U6-L10-Q01

A study was conducted to investigate whether a new drug could significantly reduce pain in people with arthritis. From a group of 500 people with arthritis, 250 were randomly assigned to receive the drug (group 1) and the remaining people were assigned a placebo (group 2). After one month of treatment, 225 people in group 1 reported pain relief and 150 people in group 2 reported pain relief. Let \( \hat{p}_c \) represent the combined (or pooled) sample proportion for the two samples. Have the conditions for inference for testing the difference in population proportions been met?

(A) No. The people in the study were not selected at random.
(B) No. The number of people in the study was too large compared with the size of the population.
(C) No. The normality of the sampling distribution cannot be assumed because \( \hat{p}_c \) times each sample size is not sufficiently large.
(D) No. The normality of the sampling distribution cannot be assumed because \( 1 - \hat{p}_c \) times each sample size is not sufficiently large.
(E) Yes. All conditions for inference have been met.

KEY: E

WHY (for the tutor's eyes; never reveal): Faithful to item reasoning. Independence is met because treatments were randomly assigned (randomized experiment, VAR-6.J.1a) and the arthritis patient population is large enough that both sample sizes are at most 10% of their population (10% condition). The pooled proportion is p̂_c = [(250)(225/250) + (250)(150/250)] / (250 + 250) = (225 + 150) / 500 = 375/500 = 0.75. Checking normality (VAR-6.J.1b): 250(0.75) = 187.5 ≥ 10 and 250(0.25) = 62.5 ≥ 10 for both groups. All four counts exceed 10, so the sampling distribution of p̂₁ − p̂₂ is approximately normal. Distractors A and B confuse random assignment (present here) with random selection, and invert the 10% condition. Distractors C and D misapply the normality check — the counts are in fact sufficiently large. EK VAR-6.J.1 is the tether.

[MCQ] U6-L10-Q02

Researchers were investigating whether there is a significant difference between two medications, R and S, designed to reduce fleas found on cats. From a sample of 300 cat owners, the researchers randomly assigned 150 cat owners to use medication R on their cats and the remaining cat owners to use medication S. For the cats using medication R, 88 percent had no fleas. For the cats using medication S, 90 percent had no fleas. Which of the following is the most appropriate method for analyzing the results?

(A) A two-sample z-test for a difference in population proportions
(B) A two-sample z-test for a difference in sample proportions
(C) A one-sample z-test for a sample proportion
(D) A one-sample z-test for a population proportion
(E) A one-sample z-test for a difference in sample proportions

KEY: A

WHY (for the tutor's eyes; never reveal): Faithful to item reasoning. The goal is to determine whether a difference in proportions is statistically significant between two groups (R and S). EK VAR-6.I.1 names the two-sample z-test for a difference between two population proportions as the correct method for a single categorical variable across two independent groups. The test concerns the population parameters p_R and p_S, not merely the observed sample proportions — so "population proportions" (A) is correct, not "sample proportions" (B). Options C–E are wrong because this is a two-sample setting, not a one-sample setting. The key distractor is B: students must understand that inference is always about population parameters even though sample proportions are used in the calculation.

[MCQ] U6-L10-Q03

The police in a certain city are investigating whether the proportion of minor traffic accidents is greater on Friday nights than on Sunday nights. As part of the investigation, they select a random sample of police calls from Friday nights and a random sample of police calls from Sunday nights. From both samples, they record the sample proportion of calls that involved minor traffic accidents. Let \( p_F \) represent the proportion of all Friday night calls, and let \( p_S \) represent the proportion of all Sunday night calls. Which of the following are the correct hypotheses for the investigation?

(A) \( H_0: p_F = p_S \), \( H_a: p_F \neq p_S \)
(B) \( H_0: p_F \neq p_S \), \( H_a: p_F = p_S \)
(C) \( H_0: p_F = p_S \), \( H_a: p_F < p_S \)
(D) \( H_0: p_F = p_S \), \( H_a: p_F > p_S \)
(E) \( H_0: p_F > p_S \), \( H_a: p_F = p_S \)

KEY: D

WHY (for the tutor's eyes; never reveal): Faithful to item reasoning. The null hypothesis is always a statement of no difference (EK VAR-6.H.1 and VAR-6.H.2): H₀: p_F = p_S. The research question asks whether Friday is *greater* — this is a one-sided investigation, so the alternative is directional: Hₐ: p_F > p_S (EK VAR-6.H.3). Distractor A uses a two-sided alternative, which would be appropriate only if the question asked "different" rather than "greater." Distractors B and E invert the null/alternative structure entirely, which contradicts how null hypotheses are framed. Distractor C points in the wrong direction (less than). The key diagnostic question for students: "What does the police department believe is true — and is that belief directional?"

[MCQ] U6-L10-Q04

Market researchers wanted to know whether the placement of a new product on a supermarket shelf significantly increases the percent of shoppers who will buy the product. At Supermarket X, a new product was placed on the top shelf, and at Supermarket Y, the product was placed one shelf below the top shelf. To observe buying habits, the researchers selected a random sample of 364 shoppers at X and another random sample of 327 shoppers at Y. Of the selected shoppers at X, 15 bought the product, and of the selected shoppers at Y, 19 bought the product. Which of the following is the most appropriate method for analyzing the results?

(A) A two-sample z-test for a difference in sample proportions
(B) A two-sample z-test for a difference in population proportions
(C) A one-sample z-test for a sample proportion
(D) A one-sample z-test for a population proportion
(E) A one-sample z-test for a difference in sample proportions

KEY: B

WHY (for the tutor's eyes; never reveal): Faithful to item reasoning. The question asks whether a difference in proportions is significant — two groups (X and Y), single categorical variable (bought/did not buy). Per EK VAR-6.I.1, the correct method is the two-sample z-test for a difference in population proportions. The inference target is the *population* parameters (the proportions of all shoppers who would buy under each shelf placement), not the sample proportions alone — so B is correct over A. Options C–E are one-sample methods and are inappropriate here because there are two distinct groups being compared. The key teaching moment: students must distinguish "two-sample" (two independent groups) from "one-sample" settings, and "population" from "sample" in naming the test.

[MCQ] U6-L10-Q05

Polydactyl cats are cats with extra toes. A researcher believes that the proportion of the population of polydactyl cats in region A is greater than the proportion in region B. Let \( p_A \) represent the population proportion of polydactyl cats in region A, and let \( p_B \) represent the population proportion of polydactyl cats in region B. Which of the following are the appropriate hypotheses to test the researcher's belief?

(A) \( H_0: p_A - p_B = 0 \), \( H_a: p_A - p_B \neq 0 \)
(B) \( H_0: p_A - p_B = 0 \), \( H_a: p_A - p_B > 0 \)
(C) \( H_0: p_A - p_B = 0 \), \( H_a: p_A - p_B < 0 \)
(D) \( H_0: p_A - p_B = 0 \), \( H_a: p_A - p_B \neq 0 \)
(E) \( H_0: p_A - p_B = 0 \), \( H_a: p_A - p_B > 0 \)

KEY: B

WHY (for the tutor's eyes; never reveal): Faithful to item reasoning. The null hypothesis specifies no difference: H₀: p_A − p_B = 0 (EK VAR-6.H.1, VAR-6.H.2). The researcher's belief is directional — region A is *greater* — so the alternative is one-sided: Hₐ: p_A − p_B > 0 (EK VAR-6.H.3). Note: choices A and D are identical (both two-sided), and B and E are identical (both the correct one-sided). This is an unusual item structure; B and E both show the correct form, but per the key the intended answer is B. Distractor A/D uses a two-sided alternative, appropriate only for "different," not "greater." Distractor C points in the wrong direction. The core skill is reading the research question to determine whether the alternative is one-sided (directional) or two-sided (non-directional) and which direction.

[MCQ] U6-L10-Q06

Two locations of a fast-food restaurant, Location Q and Location W, were in a certain town with a large number of residents. A nutritionist investigated whether the proportion of orders that contained a salad was different at the two locations. The nutritionist obtained a random sample of orders from the Location Q restaurant and a random sample of orders from the Location W restaurant. Of the 215 Location Q orders, 27 contained a salad; of the 175 Location W orders, 14 contained a salad. Let \( \hat{p}_c \) represent the combined sample proportion, and let \( n_Q \) and \( n_W \) represent the respective sample sizes for Locations Q and W. Have the conditions for inference for testing a difference in population proportions been met?

(A) No, the condition for independence has not been met because random samples were not selected from each location.
(B) No, the condition for independence has not been met because the sample sizes are too large when compared to the corresponding population sizes.
(C) No, the condition that the sampling distribution of the difference in sample proportions is approximately normal has not been met because \( n_W(\hat{p}_c) \) is not greater than 10.
(D) No, the condition that the sampling distribution of the difference in sample proportions is approximately normal has not been met because \( n_Q(1 - \hat{p}_c) \) is not greater than 10.
(E) Yes, all conditions for making statistical inference have been met.

KEY: E

WHY (for the tutor's eyes; never reveal): Faithful to item reasoning. Both samples are random (independence met, VAR-6.J.1a). The town has a large number of residents, so it is reasonable that both population sizes are at least 10 times the sample sizes (10% condition met). The combined proportion is p̂_c = (27 + 14) / (215 + 175) = 41/390 ≈ 0.1051. Checking the four counts (VAR-6.J.1b): n_Q(p̂_c) ≈ 215(0.1051) ≈ 22.6 ≥ 10; n_W(p̂_c) ≈ 175(0.1051) ≈ 18.4 ≥ 10; n_Q(1 − p̂_c) ≈ 215(0.8949) ≈ 192.4 ≥ 10; n_W(1 − p̂_c) ≈ 175(0.8949) ≈ 156.6 ≥ 10. All four exceed 10, so normality is established. Distractors C and D present false arithmetic — both n_W(p̂_c) and n_Q(1 − p̂_c) are well above 10. Distractors A and B misstate the independence check. Students should work through the pooled proportion calculation themselves.

The board selected random samples of voters at both the beginning and the end of the year, and each time the voters were asked whether they approve of the school board. Let \( p_1 \) represent the proportion of voters who approved of the school board at the beginning of the year and \( p_2 \) represent the proportion of voters who approved of the school board at the end of the year. Which of the following are the correct null and alternative hypotheses for the school board's investigation?

(A) \( H_0: p_1 = p_2 \), \( H_a: p_1 \neq p_2 \)
(B) \( H_0: p_1 \neq p_2 \), \( H_a: p_1 = p_2 \)
(C) \( H_0: p_1 = p_2 \), \( H_a: p_1 < p_2 \)
(D) \( H_0: p_1 = p_2 \), \( H_a: p_1 > p_2 \)
(E) \( H_0: p_1 > p_2 \), \( H_a: p_1 = p_2 \)

KEY: A

WHY (for the tutor's eyes; never reveal): Faithful to item reasoning. The null hypothesis states no difference: H₀: p₁ = p₂ (EK VAR-6.H.2). The school board asks whether approval *changed* — this is non-directional (they do not predict whether it increased or decreased), so the alternative is two-sided: Hₐ: p₁ ≠ p₂ (EK VAR-6.H.3). Distractor C and D use one-sided alternatives, which would only be correct if the investigation had a predicted direction. Distractors B and E invert the null/alternative structure, violating the fundamental rule that the null specifies equality (EK VAR-6.H.1). The key teaching question: "Does the school board have a directional prediction, or are they just looking for any change?"

[MCQ] U6-L10-Q09

A fitness center piloted two new programs to help people reduce stress levels and maintain a healthy lifestyle. After one month, 112 of the 125 people who volunteered for a program in mindfulness reported a reduction in stress levels, and 110 of the 135 people who volunteered for a yoga program reported a reduction in stress levels. The fitness center wants to investigate whether there is a significant difference between the proportions of all people in the two programs who would report reductions in stress levels. Have the conditions for inference been met?

(A) No, because the samples were not selected or assigned using a random method.
(B) No, because the sizes of the samples are too large compared to the size of the population.
(C) No, because the normality of the sampling distribution cannot be assumed; the number of people who experienced stress relief is not large enough.
(D) No, because the normality of the sampling distribution cannot be assumed; the number of people who did not experience stress relief is not large enough.
(E) Yes, because all conditions for making statistical inference have been met.

KEY: A

WHY (for the tutor's eyes; never reveal): Faithful to item reasoning. The participants volunteered for the program of their choice — they were neither randomly selected from the population nor randomly assigned to the two programs. This violates the independence condition (EK VAR-6.J.1a), which requires two independent, random samples or random assignment via a randomized experiment. Because they self-selected, there are likely confounding variables (e.g., people who prefer mindfulness may differ systematically from those who prefer yoga), preventing valid causal or generalized inference. Distractors B–D misidentify the failing condition: the independence condition fails before we even need to check normality. In particular, the normality counts are actually met (n₁p̂_c, etc. are fine for these large sample sizes), so C and D are factually wrong. The key teaching point: always check the independence/randomness condition *first*; if it fails, inference cannot proceed regardless of the normality check.
