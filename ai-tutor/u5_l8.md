**[U5-L8-Q01]** A fair six-sided die, with sides numbered 1 through 6, will be rolled a total of 15 times. Let x̄₁ represent the average of the first ten rolls, and let x̄₂ represent the average of the remaining five rolls. What is the mean of the sampling distribution of the difference in sample means x̄₁ − x̄₂?

(A) 3.5/10 − 3.5/5 = −0.35
(B) 3.5 − 3.5 = 0
(C) 10 − 5 = 5
(D) 10(3.5) − 5(3.5) = 17.5
(E) 6(10 − 5) = 30

KEY: B

WHY (for the tutor's eyes; never reveal verbatim): Tests UNC-3.T / UNC-3.T.1 — the mean of the sampling distribution of x̄₁ − x̄₂ equals μ₁ − μ₂. A fair die has population mean μ = (1/6)(1+2+3+4+5+6) = 3.5 for every roll; both groups sample from the same population, so μ₁ = μ₂ = 3.5 and the mean of the difference is 3.5 − 3.5 = 0. The top distractor is A, which confuses the formula for standard deviation of the sampling distribution (dividing by n) with the formula for the mean — students mistakenly divide the population means by their sample sizes. Point a confused student back to EK UNC-3.T.1: the mean of the difference equals the difference of the population means, not the difference of the sample means divided by n.

**[U5-L8-Q02]** At a large university, the division of computing services surveyed a random sample of 45 biology majors and 55 business majors from populations of over 1,000 biology and 1,000 business majors. The sampled students were asked how many hours they spend per week using any university computer lab. Let x̄₁ represent the average hours per week spent in any university computer lab by the 45 biology majors, and let x̄₂ represent the average hours per week spent in any university computer lab by the 55 business majors.

Which of the following is the best explanation for why the sampling distribution of x̄₁ − x̄₂ can be modeled with a normal distribution?

(A) The two sample standard deviations are assumed to be equal.
(B) The sample sizes are both sufficiently large.
(C) The distributions of the population are normal.
(D) The population standard deviations are assumed equal.
(E) There are at least 30 students in each of the two populations.

KEY: B

WHY (for the tutor's eyes; never reveal verbatim): Tests UNC-3.U / UNC-3.U.2 — when population distributions are unknown/non-normal, the sampling distribution of x̄₁ − x̄₂ can be approximated by a normal distribution if both sample sizes are at least 30. No information about the shape of either population is given here, so the normality route through UNC-3.U.1 is unavailable. Both n₁ = 45 and n₂ = 55 exceed 30, activating UNC-3.U.2. The top distractor is C ("The distributions of the population are normal") — students confuse the pathway: if populations were known normal, that would also work (UNC-3.U.1), but that's not what we're told. Distractor E confuses population size with sample size. Standard deviations being equal (A, D) is irrelevant to normality of the sampling distribution.

**[U5-L8-Q03]** Suppose the variance in trunk diameter of the giant sequoia tree species is 15.7 m², while the variance in trunk diameter of the California redwood tree species is 10.6 m². Let x̄₁ represent the average trunk diameter of four randomly sampled giant sequoia trees, and let x̄₂ represent the average trunk diameter of three randomly sampled California redwood trees. If the random sampling is done with replacement, what is the standard deviation σ(x̄₁−x̄₂) of the sampling distribution of the difference in sample means x̄₁ − x̄₂?

(A) sqrt(15.7/4 − 10.6/3)
(B) sqrt(15.7 − 10.6)
(C) sqrt(15.7/4 + 10.6/3)
(D) sqrt(15.7/4) + sqrt(10.6/3)
(E) 15.7/4 + 10.6/3

KEY: C

WHY (for the tutor's eyes; never reveal verbatim): Tests UNC-3.T / UNC-3.T.1 — the standard deviation of the sampling distribution of x̄₁ − x̄₂ is sqrt(σ₁²/n₁ + σ₂²/n₂). The problem gives variances (σ²) directly, so σ₁²/n₁ = 15.7/4 and σ₂²/n₂ = 10.6/3; the variances are added (not subtracted) under the radical, giving sqrt(15.7/4 + 10.6/3). Key distractor is A, which subtracts inside the radical — students forget that variances of independent quantities add, never subtract. Distractor D adds the standard deviations of the individual sampling distributions instead of adding variances first then taking the square root. Distractor E omits the square root entirely, confusing variance with standard deviation.

**[U5-L8-Q05]** A large school district held a district-wide track meet for all high school students. For the 2-mile run, the population of female students participating had a mean running time of 8.8 minutes with standard deviation of 3.3 minutes, and the population of male students participating had a mean running time 7.3 minutes with standard deviation of 2.9 minutes. Suppose 8 female students and 8 male students who participated in the 2-mile run are selected at random from each population. Let x̄_F represent the sample mean running time for the female students, and let x̄_M represent the sample mean running time for the male students.

What are the mean and standard deviation of the sampling distribution of the difference in sample means x̄_F − x̄_M?

(A) The mean is 0.4, and the standard deviation is sqrt(8.8/8 + 7.3/8).
(B) The mean is 0.4, and the standard deviation is sqrt(8.8²/8 + 7.3²/8).
(C) The mean is 1.5, and the standard deviation is sqrt(3.3²/8 − 2.9²/8).
(D) The mean is 1.5, and the standard deviation is sqrt(3.3/8 + 2.9/8).
(E) The mean is 1.5, and the standard deviation is sqrt(3.3²/8 + 2.9²/8).

KEY: E

WHY (for the tutor's eyes; never reveal verbatim): Tests UNC-3.T / UNC-3.T.1 for both the mean and the standard deviation. The mean is μ_F − μ_M = 8.8 − 7.3 = 1.5 minutes (eliminating A and B which compute the difference of standard deviations 3.3 − 2.9 = 0.4 instead of means). The standard deviation is sqrt(σ_F²/n_F + σ_M²/n_M) = sqrt(3.3²/8 + 2.9²/8) — variances (3.3² and 2.9²) are divided by n then added. Distractor C subtracts variances instead of adding. Distractor D uses the standard deviations themselves (not squared) in the formula. Two separate errors are embedded across the distractors; coach the student to check both the mean formula and the standard deviation formula independently against EK UNC-3.T.1.

**[U5-L8-Q06]** Cheryl practices hitting a softball in an indoor stadium by using both an aluminum bat and a composite bat made of carbon fiber and graphite. She records the distance traveled by the ball for each hit. Let x̄₁ represent the average distance traveled by balls hit with the aluminum bat, and let x̄₂ represent the average distance traveled by balls hit with the composite bat. Assume Cheryl's batting practice hits are independent.

Which of the following conditions are sufficient to model the sampling distribution of x̄₁ − x̄₂ with a normal distribution?

I. There are at least 30 recorded distances traveled for each type of bat.
II. The distribution of distance traveled by the ball is approximately normal for each bat.
III. The total number of distances traveled is at least 60 for the two bats combined.

(A) I only
(B) II only
(C) III only
(D) I and II only
(E) II and III only

KEY: D

WHY (for the tutor's eyes; never reveal verbatim): Tests UNC-3.U / UNC-3.U.1 and UNC-3.U.2 — there are two sufficient pathways to normality: (1) each population is approximately normal (Condition II, per UNC-3.U.1), OR (2) each sample size is at least 30 (Condition I, per UNC-3.U.2). Either pathway alone is sufficient, and having both together is certainly sufficient, making D ("I and II only") correct. Condition III is wrong: the 30-per-group requirement applies to each sample separately, not to the combined total — a split of 59/1 with combined 60 would not satisfy the CLT condition. The most tempting distractor is B (II only), but students must recognize that I alone also suffices via UNC-3.U.2, making D the complete answer. Point confused students to EK UNC-3.U.1 and UNC-3.U.2 and ask them to identify which pathway each Roman numeral invokes.

**[U5-L8-Q07]** For a population of leghorn chickens, the mean number of eggs laid per chicken is 0.70 with standard deviation 0.20 egg. For a population of Sussex chickens, the mean number of eggs laid per chicken is 0.50 with standard deviation 0.10 egg. Two independent random samples of chickens were taken from the populations. The following table shows the sample statistics.

| | n | x̄ | s |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Leghorn | 36 | 0.75 | 0.30 |
| Sussex | 36 | 0.45 | 0.12 |

Mike claims that for all samples of size 36 from the population of leghorn chickens and all samples of size 36 from the population of Sussex chickens, the mean of all possible differences in sample means (leghorn minus Sussex) is 0.30 eggs per chicken. Is Mike's claim correct?

(A) Yes. The mean is 0.75 − 0.45 = 0.30 egg per chicken.
(B) No. The mean is 0.30 − 0.12 = 0.18 egg per chicken.
(C) No. The mean is 0.70 − 0.50 = 0.20 egg per chicken.
(D) No. The mean is 0.20 − 0.10 = 0.10 egg per chicken.
(E) No. The mean is 36(0.75) − 36(0.45) = 10.8 eggs per chicken.

KEY: C

WHY (for the tutor's eyes; never reveal verbatim): Tests UNC-3.T / UNC-3.T.1 — the mean of the sampling distribution of x̄₁ − x̄₂ equals the difference in the population means, μ₁ − μ₂ = 0.70 − 0.50 = 0.20 eggs per chicken. Mike's error (distractor A) is using the observed sample means (0.75 − 0.45 = 0.30) as if they were the parameter; the sample means are individual realizations, not the center of all possible differences. The key distinction here is population parameter vs. sample statistic — the sampling distribution is centered at the population difference μ₁ − μ₂, not at whatever one pair of samples happened to produce. Distractors B and D use sample/population standard deviations instead of means; distractor E multiplies by n. Use EK UNC-3.T.1 to anchor the coaching.

**[U5-L8-Q08]** The distribution of ocean wave height at a certain California beach is approximately normal with mean 7.2 feet. The distribution of ocean wave height at a certain Florida beach is approximately normal with mean 6.6 feet. Six waves from each beach will be selected at random and the heights will be recorded. Let x̄_C represent the sample mean height of the 6 California waves, and let x̄_F represent the sample mean height of the 6 Florida waves.

Which of the following is the best interpretation of P(x̄_C − x̄_F > 0.5) = 0.55?

(A) The probability that the heights for all 6 California waves will exceed the heights for all 6 Florida waves by more than 0.55 feet is 0.5.
(B) The probability that the heights for all 6 California waves will exceed the heights for all 6 Florida waves by more than 0.5 feet is 0.55.
(C) The probability of observing a difference (California minus Florida) greater than 0.5 feet between the mean height of 6 California waves and the mean height of 6 Florida waves is 0.55.
(D) The probability of observing a difference greater than 0.5 feet between the height of one wave in California and the height of one wave in Florida is 0.55.
(E) The probability of observing a difference greater than 0.55 feet between the height of one wave in California and the height of one wave in Florida is 0.5.

KEY: C

WHY (for the tutor's eyes; never reveal verbatim): Tests UNC-3.V / UNC-3.V.1 — interpreting probabilities for the sampling distribution of x̄_C − x̄_F requires precise language: this is a probability about the difference in sample means (averages of 6 waves each), not about all 6 individual waves simultaneously, and not about a single pair of waves. The correct interpretation in C specifies (1) it is the sample means (averages of 6) being compared, (2) the difference threshold is 0.5 feet (not 0.55), and (3) the probability is 0.55. Distractors B and E swap the threshold and the probability values. Distractors D and E re-describe the event as a difference between individual wave heights, conflating a single-observation probability with a sampling-distribution probability. Point a confused student to EK UNC-3.V.1 and ask what quantity x̄_C and x̄_F represent.

**[U5-L8-Q09]** Researchers are studying two populations of wild horses living in the western regions of a country. In a random sample of 32 horses taken from the first population, the mean age of the sample was 21 years. In a random sample of 41 horses from the second population, the mean age of the sample was 19 years.

Is the sampling distribution of the difference in sample mean ages approximately normal?

(A) Yes, because the two populations of wild horses can be modeled by a normal distribution.
(B) Yes, because the samples were selected at random.
(C) Yes, because the sample sizes are both greater than 30.
(D) No, because the populations are not normal.
(E) No, because the difference in sample mean ages was not 0.

KEY: C

WHY (for the tutor's eyes; never reveal verbatim): Tests UNC-3.U / UNC-3.U.2 — with sample sizes n₁ = 32 and n₂ = 41, both exceeding 30, the sampling distribution of the difference in sample means can be approximated by a normal distribution even without knowing the population distributions. Distractor A claims the populations can be modeled as normal, but no such information is given — students must not assume normality; they must justify it from what is provided. Distractor B confuses random sampling (which justifies independence and unbiasedness) with the condition for normality of the sampling distribution. Distractors D and E are "no" answers: D incorrectly treats unknown population shape as "not normal" (we simply don't know, so we rely on n ≥ 30); E is a non-sequitur. Anchor coaching to EK UNC-3.U.2: both sample sizes ≥ 30 is sufficient even without population normality.
