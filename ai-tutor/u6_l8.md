**[MCQ] U6-L8-Q01**
There are 1,000 golden delicious and 1,000 red delicious apples in a cooler. In a random sample of 75 of the golden delicious apples, 48 had blemishes. In a random sample of 75 of the red delicious apples, 42 had blemishes. Assume all conditions for inference have been met. Which of the following is closest to the interval estimate of the difference in the numbers of apples with blemishes (golden delicious minus red delicious) at a 98 percent level of confidence?

(A) \((-0.076, 0.236)\)
(B) \((-0.105, 0.265)\)
(C) \((-10.5, 26.5)\)
(D) \((-76, 236)\)
(E) \((-105, 265)\)

KEY: E

WHY (for the tutor's eyes; never reveal): The item tests EK UNC-4.L.1 — converting a CI for a difference in proportions into an interval estimate with specified units (counts). First, the 98% CI for the difference in proportions (golden minus red) is (−0.105, 0.265). Because there are 1,000 apples of each type, multiply the proportion endpoints by 1,000 to get the interval for the difference in numbers: (−0.105 × 1000, 0.265 × 1000) = (−105, 265). Choice B gives the proportion interval (not yet scaled), and Choices C and D reflect wrong scale factors (dividing or using 100 instead of 1000). The key distinction is LO UNC-4.L: the CI for proportions must be scaled by population size to yield an interval estimate in counts.

**[MCQ] U6-L8-Q02**
Two large containers, X and Y, contain many colored beads. From a random sample of beads taken from container X, the proportion of blue beads in the sample was recorded as \(\hat{p}_X = 0.35\). From a random sample of beads taken from container Y, the proportion of blue beads in the sample was recorded as \(\hat{p}_Y = 0.39\). Assuming all conditions for inference are met, which of the following procedures is the most appropriate for estimating the difference between the proportions of all blue beads in the containers?

(A) A two-sample \(z\)-interval for a difference in population proportions
(B) A two-sample \(z\)-interval for a difference in sample proportions
(C) A one-sample \(z\)-interval for a population proportion
(D) A one-sample \(z\)-interval for a sample proportion
(E) A one-sample \(z\)-interval for a difference in population proportions

KEY: A

WHY (for the tutor's eyes; never reveal): This item tests EK UNC-4.I.1 and LO UNC-4.I (Skill 1.D) — identifying the correct procedure. A two-sample z-interval is appropriate for estimating the difference between two population proportions when there are independent random samples from two distinct populations. The parameter of interest is a population parameter (the true difference), not a sample statistic — so "difference in sample proportions" (B) is incorrect language for a parameter. Choices C, D, E are one-sample procedures and cannot address a two-population comparison.

(A) Construct a two-sample \(z\)-interval for the difference between population proportions.
(B) Construct a two-sample \(z\)-interval for the difference between sample proportions.
(C) Perform a \(z\)-test for the difference in sample proportions.
(D) Subtract the proportions and construct a one-sample \(z\)-interval for a single population proportion.

KEY: A

WHY (for the tutor's eyes; never reveal): This item again tests LO UNC-4.I and EK UNC-4.I.1 (Skill 1.D) — the two-sample z-interval estimates the true difference between population proportions (the parameter of interest), not merely the sample statistics. Choice B uses "sample proportions" as the parameter, which is a category error — inference targets population parameters. Choice C is a significance test, not a confidence interval. Choices D and E collapse two samples into a single one-sample procedure, which is incorrect methodology when two independent samples are present.

**[MCQ] U6-L8-Q04**
Which of the following is not a condition for constructing a confidence interval to estimate the difference between two population proportions?

(A) The samples must be selected randomly.
(B) The data must come from populations with approximately normal distributions.
(C) When samples are taken without replacement, each population must be at least 10 times as large as its corresponding sample.
(D) The samples must be independent of each other.
(E) The observed number of successes and failures for both samples must be at least 10.

KEY: B

WHY (for the tutor's eyes; never reveal): This item tests EK UNC-4.J.1 and LO UNC-4.J (Skill 4.C). Distributions of categorical data cannot be normally distributed — normality is not a condition placed on the original population data. The condition that must be checked is that the sampling distribution of the difference in sample proportions is approximately normal, which is verified by checking that n1*p̂1, n1*(1−p̂1), n2*p̂2, and n2*(1−p̂2) are all at least 10 (choice E). Randomness (A), independence/10% rule for sampling without replacement (C), and sample independence (D) are genuine conditions from EK UNC-4.J.1. Choice B conflates the normality condition for quantitative data with the large-counts condition for categorical data.

(B) Both populations must be approximately normal.

**[MCQ] U6-L8-Q07**
A gardener wants to know if soaking seeds in water before planting them increases the proportion of seeds that germinate. To investigate, the gardener will assign 50 seeds to be soaked before planting and 50 seeds to be planted without being soaked. After two weeks, the gardener will record how many seeds in each group germinated and construct a 95 percent confidence interval for the difference in proportions. Which of the following conditions for inference should be met?

I. The seeds should be randomly assigned to a treatment.
II. The group sizes should be less than 10 percent of the population sizes.
III. The number of successful seeds and unsuccessful seeds in each group should be at least 10.

(A) I only
(B) II only
(C) III only
(D) I and III only
(E) I, II, and III

KEY: D

WHY (for the tutor's eyes; never reveal): This item tests EK UNC-4.J.1 (Skill 4.C) in an experimental design context. Because this is a randomized experiment (not sampling from a population), random assignment to treatments (Condition I) is necessary to support valid inference and causal conclusions. The large-counts condition (Condition III) is also required to verify that the sampling distribution of the difference in sample proportions is approximately normal. Condition II (the 10% rule) applies when sampling without replacement from a finite population; in a controlled experiment with a fixed number of seeds assigned to groups, this condition is not applicable. The reasoning field confirms: the 10% rule "is not necessary here because this is an experimental design with finite seed populations, not sampling from infinite populations."

**[MCQ] U6-L8-Q08**
Researchers are studying the distribution of subscribers to a certain streaming service in different populations. From a random sample of 200 people in City C, 34 were found to subscribe to the streaming service. From a random sample of 200 people in City K, 54 were found to subscribe to the streaming service. Assuming all conditions for inference are met, which of the following is a 90 percent confidence interval for the difference in population proportions (City C minus City K) who subscribe to the streaming service?

(A) \((0.17 - 0.27) \pm 1.65\sqrt{\frac{0.17}{200} + \frac{0.27}{200}}\)
(B) \((0.17 - 0.27) \pm 1.96\sqrt{\frac{(0.17)(0.83)+(0.27)(0.73)}{400}}\)
(C) \((0.17 - 0.27) \pm 1.65\sqrt{\frac{(0.17)(0.83)+(0.27)(0.73)}{400}}\)
(D) \((0.17 - 0.27) \pm 1.96\sqrt{\frac{(0.17)(0.83)+(0.27)(0.73)}{200}}\)
(E) \((0.17 - 0.27) \pm 1.65\sqrt{\frac{(0.17)(0.83)+(0.27)(0.73)}{200}}\)

KEY: E

WHY (for the tutor's eyes; never reveal): This item tests EK UNC-4.K.1 (Skill 3.D) — constructing the two-sample z-interval. The sample proportions are p̂_C = 34/200 = 0.17 and p̂_K = 54/200 = 0.27. For 90% confidence the critical value is z* = 1.65. The correct formula is (p̂1 − p̂2) ± z* · sqrt(p̂1(1−p̂1)/n1 + p̂2(1−p̂2)/n2), which gives (0.17 − 0.27) ± 1.65 · sqrt((0.17)(0.83)/200 + (0.27)(0.73)/200). Choice A omits the (1 − p̂) factor in the SE. Choices B and D use z* = 1.96 (the 95% critical value, not 90%). Choices B, C, and D pool the variances over 400 (the combined sample size) rather than keeping each term over its own n = 200, which is incorrect for the two-sample SE formula.

**[MCQ] U6-L8-Q09**
From a random sample of 185 children from school G, 108 indicated they wanted to study science in college. From a different random sample of 165 children from school H, 92 indicated they wanted to study science in college. Assuming all conditions for inference are met, which of the following is closest to the standard error for a confidence interval for the difference in population proportions between the two schools of children who want to study science in college?

(A) \(\sqrt{\frac{\frac{200}{350}(1-\frac{200}{350})}{350}}\)
(B) \(1.96\sqrt{\frac{\frac{200}{350}(1-\frac{200}{350})}{350}}\)
(C) \(\sqrt{\frac{\frac{108}{185}(1-\frac{108}{185})}{185} - \frac{\frac{92}{165}(1-\frac{92}{165})}{165}}\)
(D) \(\sqrt{\frac{\frac{108}{185}(1-\frac{108}{185})}{185} + \frac{\frac{92}{165}(1-\frac{92}{165})}{165}}\)
(E) \(1.96\sqrt{\frac{\frac{108}{185}(1-\frac{108}{185})}{185} + \frac{\frac{92}{165}(1-\frac{92}{165})}{165}}\)

KEY: D

WHY (for the tutor's eyes; never reveal): This item tests EK UNC-4.K.1 (Skill 3.D) — specifically the standard error component of the two-sample z-interval formula. The SE is sqrt(p̂1(1−p̂1)/n1 + p̂2(1−p̂2)/n2), with p̂1 = 108/185, n1 = 185, p̂2 = 92/165, n2 = 165. Choices A and B incorrectly pool the two samples into a single combined proportion (200/350 over 350), treating the problem as one-sample. Choice C uses subtraction inside the square root instead of addition — variances of independent quantities add, they do not subtract. Choice E includes the z* = 1.96 multiplier, which makes it the margin of error at 95% confidence, not the standard error alone. Only D correctly adds the two separate variance terms under the radical using each group's own sample proportion and sample size.
