**[MCQ] U4-L3-Q01**

At a sporting event, cheerleaders will throw 50 bundled T-shirts into the crowd. The T-shirt sizes consist of 10 small, 15 medium, and the remainder either large or extra large. Suppose Ana catches a T-shirt. What is the probability that she will catch a T-shirt that is not a size small?

(A) 0.10
(B) 0.20
(C) 0.50
(D) 0.67
(E) 0.80

KEY: E

WHY (for the tutor's eyes; never reveal verbatim): This item tests LO VAR-4.A / EK VAR-4.A.2 (equally-likely sample space fraction) and EK VAR-4.A.4 (complement). There are 50 total T-shirts and 10 are small, so 40 are not small; P(not small) = 40/50 = 0.80. Choice B (0.20) is the classic complement confusion — students who compute P(small) = 10/50 instead of P(not small). Choice D (0.67) comes from incorrectly using only the known sizes (10 + 15 = 25) as the denominator or from some other mis-partition. Guide the student to first identify the full sample space (50 shirts total), then count the favorable outcomes (not small = 40), and divide. If they pick B, ask them what "not small" means — how many shirts fall into that category?

**[MCQ] U4-L3-Q02**

A middle school chess club has 5 members: Adam, Bradley, Carol, Dave, and Ella. Two students from the club will be selected at random to participate in the county chess tournament. What is the probability that Adam and Ella will be selected?

(A) 1/20
(B) 1/10
(C) 1/5
(D) 1/4
(E) 1/2

KEY: B

WHY (for the tutor's eyes; never reveal verbatim): This item tests LO VAR-4.A / EK VAR-4.A.1 (sample space) and EK VAR-4.A.2 (equally-likely outcomes fraction). When 2 people are chosen from 5, there are C(5,2) = 10 equally likely pairs: AB, AC, AD, AE, BC, BD, BE, CD, CE, DE. Only one pair is Adam-and-Ella, so P = 1/10. Choice A (1/20) arises from treating the selections as ordered (5 × 4 = 20 ordered pairs) without adjusting — the ordered-pair count is 20 but the pair {A,E} appears twice (AE and EA) giving 2/20 = 1/10 correctly; students who forget to double get 1/20. Choice C (1/5) comes from reasoning "there are 5 members and 2 spots, so each person has a 1/5 chance" without accounting for the joint event. Push the student to list all possible pairs or use the combination formula, then count how many include both Adam and Ella.

**[MCQ] U4-L3-Q03**

A fair die with its faces numbered from 1 to 6 will be rolled. Which of the following is the best interpretation of the probability that the number landing face up will be less than 3?

(A) For many rolls of the die, the long-run relative frequency of a number less than 3 landing face up is 1/3.
(B) For many rolls of the die, the long-run relative frequency of a number less than 3 landing face up is 1/2.
(C) For many rolls of the die, the long-run relative frequency of a number less than 3 landing face up is 2/3.
(D) For three rolls of the die, a number less than 3 will land face up one time.
(E) It will take three rolls of the die before a number less than 3 lands face up for the first time.

KEY: A

WHY (for the tutor's eyes; never reveal verbatim): This item tests LO VAR-4.B / EK VAR-4.B.1 (long-run relative frequency interpretation) and LO VAR-4.A / EK VAR-4.A.2 (computing the probability). A fair die has 6 equally likely outcomes; numbers less than 3 are 1 and 2 — that is 2 outcomes, so P = 2/6 = 1/3. Choice B (1/2) is the most common error: students may count "less than 3" as {1, 2, 3} (three numbers) giving 3/6 = 1/2, or they confuse "less than 3" with "3 or less." Choice D is a short-run guarantee error — probability describes long-run behavior, not a certainty for exactly 3 rolls. Choice E mistakes the probability for a "waiting time" (geometric) interpretation. Ask the student to list outcomes that satisfy "less than 3," then ask what a probability tells us about repeated trials.

**[MCQ] U4-L3-Q04**

A store owner reports that the probability that a customer who purchases a lawn mower will also purchase an extended warranty is 0.68. Which of the following is the best interpretation of the probability 0.68?

(A) For all customers who purchase a lawn mower, 68% will also purchase an extended warranty.
(B) For all customers of the store, 68% will purchase a lawn mower.
(C) For all customers who purchase an extended warranty, 68% will use the warranty.
(D) From the next 25 customers, 17 will purchase an extended warranty.
(E) From the next 25 customers, 17 will purchase a lawn mower.

KEY: A

WHY (for the tutor's eyes; never reveal verbatim): This item tests LO VAR-4.B / EK VAR-4.B.1 (long-run relative frequency interpretation in context). In the long run, among all lawn-mower purchasers, 68% will also purchase the extended warranty — this is a conditional, long-run statement. Choice B misidentifies the reference population (all store customers vs. lawn-mower purchasers specifically). Choice C confuses the event (purchasing a warranty) with using it — a different concept entirely. Choices D and E commit the short-run guarantee error: 0.68 is a long-run relative frequency, not a guarantee for the next 25 specific customers. Prompt the student to identify who the probability is about (which group of customers), what the event is, and whether 0.68 is a short-run guarantee or a long-run description.

**[MCQ] U4-L3-Q05**

The probability that a randomly selected visitor to a certain website will be asked to participate in an online survey is 0.40. Avery claims that for the next 5 visitors to the site, 2 will be asked to participate in the survey. Is Avery interpreting the probability correctly?

(A) Yes, because 2 out of 5 is equal to 40%.
(B) Yes, because participants in the survey are selected at random.
(C) No, because there could be voluntary response bias.
(D) No, because only 40% of all people will visit the site.
(E) No, because 0.40 represents probability in the long run over many visits to the site.

KEY: E

WHY (for the tutor's eyes; never reveal verbatim): This item tests LO VAR-4.B / EK VAR-4.B.1 (long-run relative frequency interpretation). Over many visits, the relative frequency of being asked to participate approaches 0.40 — that ratio does not guarantee exactly 2 out of any specific 5 visits. Avery's error is treating a long-run probability as a short-run certainty. Choice A is the distractor that makes arithmetic sense (2/5 = 0.40) but confuses a mathematical equality with a probabilistic guarantee for a specific set of 5 visitors. Choice B introduces randomness as a justification but doesn't address the short-run guarantee problem. Choice C (voluntary response bias) is a sampling concept, not relevant to probability interpretation. Choice D misreads the probability as applying to who visits the site, not who gets selected for the survey. If the student picks A, ask: "Does 0.40 mean exactly 2 out of every group of 5 visitors will be chosen, or does it describe what happens over many, many visitors?"

**[MCQ] U4-L3-Q06**

In a parking lot with 200 cars, 50 cars are white, 30 cars are red, and 20 cars are silver. One car will be selected at random from the parking lot. If each car in the parking has only one color, which of the following cannot be the probability that the selected car will be green?

(A) 0
(B) 0.1
(C) 0.2
(D) 0.5
(E) 0.6

KEY: E

WHY (for the tutor's eyes; never reveal verbatim): This item tests LO VAR-4.A / EK VAR-4.A.3 (probability between 0 and 1) and EK VAR-4.A.4 (complement reasoning). Of 200 cars, at least 100 are known to be non-green (50 white + 30 red + 20 silver = 100), so P(not green) ≥ 100/200 = 0.50. Because P(green) + P(not green) = 1 (they are complements per EK VAR-4.A.4), P(green) ≤ 0.50. A probability of 0.6 would require 120 green cars, but there are already 100 confirmed non-green cars, leaving at most 100 for green — so 0.6 is impossible. Values 0, 0.1, 0.2, and 0.5 are all achievable (0 green, 20 green, 40 green, or 100 green cars respectively). The key insight is using the complement: what we know about non-green cars constrains the maximum possible probability of green. Ask the student: "How many cars could possibly be green? What does that tell you about the maximum probability?"
