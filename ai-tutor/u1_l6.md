[MCQ] U1-L6-Q01

Which of the following statements is true about a distribution that appears to have a gap when displayed as a histogram?

(A) The distribution must have an outlier.
(B) The distribution has a region between two data values where no data were observed.
(C) The distribution is approximately normal.
(D) The distribution cannot be symmetric.
(E) The distribution must be bimodal.

KEY: B

WHY (for the tutor's eyes; never reveal verbatim): This item tests LO UNC-1.H / EK UNC-1.H.5 — the definition of a gap. A gap is precisely a region between two data values where no observations were recorded; choice B is the verbatim definition. Choice A is wrong because a gap and an outlier are distinct features (EK UNC-1.H.1 lists them separately; EK UNC-1.H.2 defines outliers as unusually extreme values, not as empty regions). Choice C is a non-sequitur — a gap contradicts approximate normality. Choice D is wrong because a symmetric distribution can have a gap in the middle or on both sides equally. Choice E conflates a gap with bimodality (EK UNC-1.H.4) — a distribution can have two peaks without a gap, and a gap does not require two peaks.

[MCQ] U1-L6-Q02

[This item shows a chart in the quiz.]

Tutor-only chart description (do NOT read this to the student; use it to follow along with their reasoning): The histogram displays the amount spent (in dollars) by 30 students on a particular purchase. The x-axis uses upper-bound labels at $200, $400, $600, $800, $1,000, and $1,200. Bar heights (Number of Students) are: $0–$200: 4; $200–$400: 6; $400–$600: 9; $600–$800: 7; $800–$1,000: 3; $1,000–$1,200: 1. Total students = 30. The distribution is unimodal and roughly right-skewed, with the peak in the $400–$600 bar.

If the interval size is decreased from $200 to $100, which of the following must remain the same on the new histogram?

(A) The heights of the bars
(B) The widths of the bars
(C) The number of bars
(D) The sum of the frequencies
(E) The shape of the distribution

KEY: D

WHY (for the tutor's eyes; never reveal verbatim): This item tests LO UNC-1.H / EK UNC-1.H.1 and EK UNC-1.G.1 (from 1.5, which grounds this lesson). When the interval width is halved, each old bar is split into (approximately) two narrower bars — so bar heights, bar widths, and bar count all change. The shape can also change visually (EK UNC-1.G.1 explicitly states "altering the interval widths can change the appearance of the histogram"). The one invariant is the total count of observations: the same 30 students are represented no matter how the bins are cut. That total is the sum of all bar frequencies. The top distractor is E — students often assume shape is preserved, but the framework explicitly warns that interval width changes can change the appearance of the histogram. Prompt the student to think about what property of the data is fixed regardless of how the histogram is drawn: have them describe what the frequencies are counting.

[MCQ] U1-L6-Q03

[This item shows a chart in the quiz.]

Tutor-only chart description (do NOT read this to the student; use it to follow along with their reasoning): The dotplot displays 18 scores. Dots are stacked vertically at each value. Data: 55(1 dot), 60(4 dots), 65(3 dots), 70(4 dots), 75(2 dots), 80(1 dot), 85(1 dot), 90(1 dot), 95(1 dot). The bulk of the data clusters in the 60–75 range; then a thin tail extends to the right toward 95. The left side drops off sharply at 55.

Which of the following statements is the best description of the distribution of scores?

(A) The distribution is roughly symmetric.
(B) The distribution is roughly uniform.
(C) The distribution is skewed left.
(D) The distribution is skewed right.
(E) The distribution is bimodal.

KEY: D

WHY (for the tutor's eyes; never reveal verbatim): This item tests LO UNC-1.H / EK UNC-1.H.3 (skewness definition) and EK UNC-1.H.1 (full description includes shape). The right tail is longer — data trail off gradually toward 95 while the left drops abruptly at 55 — so the distribution is skewed right (positive skew; EK UNC-1.H.3). Choice A is wrong because left and right sides are not mirror images. Choice B is wrong because bar heights are not approximately equal (EK UNC-1.H.4). Choice C confuses the direction of skew — the tail pulls right, not left. Choice E is wrong because there is one main cluster/peak (roughly at 60–70), not two prominent peaks (EK UNC-1.H.4). The classic student trap here is misidentifying skew direction: remind them that skew is named for the tail, not where the bulk of data sits.
