**[U4-L2-Q01] MCQ**

An online customer service department estimates that about 15 percent of callers have to wait more than 8 minutes to have their calls answered by a person. The department conducted a simulation of 1,000 trials to estimate the probabilities that a certain number of callers out of the next 10 callers will have to wait more than 8 minutes to have their calls answered. The simulation is shown in the following histogram. Based on the simulation, what is the probability that at most 2 of the next 10 callers will have to wait more than 8 minutes to have their calls answered?

[This item shows a chart in the quiz.]

Tutor-only chart description (do not read aloud or compute for the student — ask them to describe what they see): The histogram's x-axis is labeled "Number of Callers" with bars at integer values 0 through 7. The y-axis is labeled "Relative Frequency" and runs from 0 to 0.36 in increments of 0.05. The relative frequency for each bar is: 0 callers = 0.181, 1 caller = 0.345, 2 callers = 0.284, 3 callers = 0.142, 4 callers = 0.039, 5 callers = 0.008, 6 callers = 0, 7 callers = 0.001. Point labels are shown on the bars. Have the student read the relevant bar heights and add them — do not compute for the student.

(A) 0.150
(B) 0.190
(C) 0.474
(D) 0.526
(E) 0.810

KEY: E

WHY (for the tutor's eyes; never reveal verbatim): "At most 2" is an event (EK UNC-2.A.3) — a collection of outcomes — meaning 0, 1, or 2 callers waited more than 8 minutes. By EK UNC-2.A.5, relative frequencies from the simulation estimate the probability of each outcome. The student must sum the three relevant bars: 0.181 + 0.345 + 0.284 = 0.810. Common distractor traps: (A) 0.150 is just the stated 15% probability, ignoring the simulation entirely; (C) and (D) suggest adding only some bars or misreading "at most 2" as "exactly 2" or "more than 2." Push them back to EK UNC-2.A.3 to re-define the event, then EK UNC-2.A.5 to add the correct relative frequencies.

**[U4-L2-Q02] MCQ**

Mateo plays on his school basketball team. From past history, he knows that his probability of making a basket on a free throw is 0.8. Suppose he wants to create a simulation using random numbers to estimate the probability of making at least 3 baskets on his next 5 free throw attempts. Which of the following assignments of the digits 0 to 9 could be used for the simulation?

(A) Let the even digits represent making a basket and the odd digits represent not making a basket.
(B) Let the digits 0 and 1 represent making a basket and the digits from 2 to 9 represent not making a basket.
(C) Let the digits from 0 to 3 represent making a basket and the digits from 4 to 9 represent not making a basket.
(D) Let the digits from 0 to 6 represent making a basket and the digits from 7 to 9 represent not making a basket.
(E) Let the digits from 0 to 7 represent making a basket and the digits 8 and 9 represent not making a basket.

KEY: E

WHY (for the tutor's eyes; never reveal verbatim): By EK UNC-2.A.4, simulation requires associating all possible outcomes with a value determined by chance, with the proportion of outcomes assigned to each result matching the real-world probability. Mateo's probability of making a basket is 0.8. Digits 0–9 give 10 equally likely values; 8 out of 10 must represent "making a basket." Only answer (E) assigns digits 0–7 (8 digits) to "making," giving 8/10 = 0.8 — an exact match. Distractor analysis: (A) assigns 5 even digits = 0.5, not 0.8; (B) assigns 2 digits = 0.2; (C) assigns 4 digits = 0.4; (D) assigns 7 digits = 0.7. If a student picks (D), they are off by one digit — ask them to count carefully and compare the resulting proportion to Mateo's stated probability (LO UNC-2.A).

**[U4-L2-Q03] MCQ**

Joslyn performed an experiment using a die with its faces numbered from 1 to 6. She rolled the die and recorded whether the 5 landed face up. She repeated the process many times and kept a cumulative record of the total number of rolls and the total number of 5s landing face up. The following table shows part of her record. Suppose Joslyn could roll the die 10,000 times and keep a record of the total number of 5s landing face up in the 10,000 rolls. What would such a record illustrate?

| Total Number of Rolls | Total Number of 5s |
|---|---|
| 5 | 1 |
| 10 | 2 |
| 15 | 2 |
| 20 | 3 |

(A) The conditional probability rule
(B) The multiplication rule
(C) The addition rule
(D) The law of large numbers
(E) The property of mutually exclusive events

KEY: D

WHY (for the tutor's eyes; never reveal verbatim): As the number of repetitions increases, the relative frequency of the 5 landing face up (e.g., 1/5, 2/10, 2/15, 3/20) will approach the true theoretical probability of 1/6 ≈ 0.167. This is precisely EK UNC-2.A.6 — the law of large numbers: simulated (empirical) probabilities tend to get closer to the true probability as the number of trials increases. With 10,000 rolls the cumulative relative frequency would stabilize near 1/6. The other choices name specific probability rules (conditional, multiplication, addition — all irrelevant here) or a property of events (mutually exclusive — also irrelevant). If a student picks (A), (B), or (C), ask them what each of those rules is about and whether any of them describe what happens as trials accumulate — steering back to EK UNC-2.A.6.
